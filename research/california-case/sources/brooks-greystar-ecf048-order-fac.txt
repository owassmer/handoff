 1         II.    LEGAL STANDARD
 2                A.    Motion to Dismiss
 3         Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) permits a party to raise by motion the
 4   defense that the complaint “fail[s] to state a claim upon which relief can be granted,”
 5   generally referred to as a motion to dismiss. The Court evaluates whether a complaint
 6   states a cognizable legal theory and sufficient facts in light of Federal Rule of Civil
 7   Procedure 8(a), which requires a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that the
 8   pleader is entitled to relief.” Although Rule 8 “does not require ‘detailed factual
 9   allegations,’” it does require “more than an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-
10   harmed-me accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl.
11   Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)). A “formulaic recitation of the elements of
12   a cause of action” is insufficient. Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555). “Nor does a
13   complaint suffice if it tenders ‘naked assertion[s]’ devoid of ‘further factual
14   enhancement.’” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 557).
15         In reviewing the plausibility of a complaint, courts “accept factual allegations in
16   the complaint as true and construe the pleadings in the light most favorable to the
17   nonmoving party.” Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co., 519 F.3d 1025, 1031
18   (9th Cir. 2008). Nonetheless, courts do not “accept as true allegations that are merely
19   conclusory, unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable inferences.” In re Gilead
20   Scis. Secs. Litig., 536 F.3d 1049, 1055 (9th Cir. 2008) (quoting Sprewell v. Golden State
21   Warriors, 266 F.3d 979, 988 (9th Cir. 2001)). The Court also need not accept as true
22   allegations that contradict matter properly subject to judicial notice or allegations
23   contradicting the exhibits attached to the complaint. Sprewell, 266 F.3d at 988.
24         “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual
25   matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Id.
26   (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). A claim is facially plausible when the facts pleaded
27   “allow[] the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
28   misconduct alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556). That is

 1   not to say that the claim must be probable, but there must be “more than a sheer
 2   possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully.” Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).
 3         When a motion to dismiss is granted, “leave to amend should be granted ‘unless
 4   the court determines that the allegation of other facts consistent with the challenged
 5   pleading could not possibly cure the deficiency.’” DeSoto v. Yellow Freight Sys., Inc.,
 6   957 F.2d 655, 658 (9th Cir. 1992) (quoting Schreiber Distrib. Co. v. Serv-Well Furniture
 7   Co., 806 F.2d 1393, 1401 (9th Cir. 1986)). The Court may deny leave to amend where an
 8   amendment would be futile. Desoto, 957 F.2d at 658 (citation omitted).
 9                B.    Motion to Strike
10         Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 19 provides that a court “may strike from a
11   pleading an insufficient defense or any redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous
12   matter.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 19(f). “’Redundant’ allegations are those that are needlessly
13   repetitive or wholly foreign to the issues involved in the action.” California Dep’t of
14   Toxic Substances Control v. Alco Pac., Inc., 217 F. Supp. 2d 1028, 1033 (C.D. Cal. 2002)
15   (citation omitted). An “immaterial” matter “has no essential or important relationship to
16   the claim for relief or the defenses being pleaded.” Fantasy, Inc. v. Fogerty, 984 F.2d
17   1524, 1527 (9th Cir. 1993), rev’d, on other grounds by Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc.,
18   510 U.S. 517 (1994) (citation omitted). “Impertinent” statements “do not pertain, and are
19   not necessary, to the issues in question.” Id. (citation omitted). “Scandalous” matter casts
20   “a cruelly derogatory light on a party or other person.” In re 2TheMart.com, Inc. Sec.
21   Litig., 114 F. Supp. 2d 955, 965 (C.D. Cal. 2000). “[M]otions to strike are generally not
22   granted unless it is clear that the matter sought to be stricken could have no possible
23   bearing on the subject matter of the litigation.” Gaines v. AT&T Mobility Servs., LLC,
24   424 F. Supp. 3d 1004, 1014 (S.D. Cal. 2019) (citation omitted).

 8                B.    Necessary and Indispensable Parties
 9         Defendants contend that Entity Defendants—voluntary dismissed by Plaintiffs—
10   are necessary parties per Rule 19(a) because they are the title owners and landlords of the

16         (A) in that person’s absence, the court cannot accord complete relief among

17

18         so situated that disposing of the action in the person’s absence may:
                  (i) as a practical matter impair or impede the person’s ability to
19
           protect the interest; or
20                (ii) leave an existing party subject to a substantial risk of incurring
           double, multiple, or otherwise inconsistent obligations because of the
21
           interest.
22   Fed. R. Civ. P. 19(a)(1). Failure to join a necessary party under Rule 19 is grounds for a
23   motion to dismiss. Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(7). If the party is necessary and cannot be joined,
24   “the court must determine whether, in equity and good conscience, the action should

13          First, Defendants argue that Entity Defendants are necessary and indispensable
14    because they are parties to the lease contracts with tenants. ECF No. 35-1 at 18, 31.
15    Defendants further argue that this action involves contractual rights because it “seeks to
16    enjoin enforcement of the security deposit provisions in the owners’ leases and to return
17    money that was paid to the owners pursuant to those leases.” ECF No. 42 at 8.
18    Defendants rely upon case law finding that all parties to a contract are necessary and
19    indispensable in any action challenging the contract’s validity or its terms, enjoining its
20    performance, or determining rights and obligations of the contract. ECF No. 35-1 at 18,
21    31–32; see, e.g., Lomayaktewa v. Hathaway, 520 F.2d 1324, 1325 (9th Cir. 1975)
22    (“No procedural principle is more deeply imbedded in the common law than that, in an
23    action to set aside a lease or a contract, all parties who may be affected by the
24    determination of the action are indispensable.” (citations omitted)).
25          The Court does not find Entity Defendants are necessary parties based solely on
26    their status as parties to the lease agreements with Plaintiffs. Unlike the cases relied on by
27    Defendants, Plaintiffs do not seek to enjoin, invalidate, or interpret the contracts, or
28    allege breach of contract. See Disabled Rts. Action Comm. v. Las Vegas Events, Inc., 375

22    Defendants will not be enjoined in this action by their status as parties to the lease
23    agreements. See Disabled Rts. Action Comm., 375 F.3d at 881. Defendants also argue that
24    one of Plaintiffs’ demands for relief is for a declaration that all “outstanding balances
25    alleged owed by the Plaintiff Class members are extinguished,” but that those are debts
26    owed to the absent Entity Defendants, not Greystar. Id.; ECF No. 10 at 89.

18                 D.     Class Allegations
19          Defendants move to strike class allegations, arguing that the facts alleged fail to
20    meet Rule 23’s requirements of commonality, typicality, adequacy, predominance,
21    superiority, and manageability for class actions. ECF No. 25-1 at 22–29; see also Fed. R.
22    Civ. P. 23(a), (b)(3). Plaintiffs argue that a motion to strike class allegations at this early
23    stage—prior to discovery—is premature. ECF No. 39 at 18–19. Plaintiffs further argue
24    that the FAC satisfies the requirements of Rule 23 for certifying a class action. Id.
25    at 21–27.
26          According to Rule 23(c), “[a]t an early practicable time after a person sues or is
27    sued as a class representative, the court must determine by order whether to certify the
28    action as a class action.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(c)(1)(A). Courts read this as “a mechanism

                                                    14
                                                                                      23cv1729-LL-VET
     Case 3:23-cv-01729-LL-VET      Document 48 Filed 09/26/24       PageID.1730     Page 15
                                             of 21


 1    for exercising defective class allegations before discovery.” Sanders v. Apple Inc., 672
 2    F. Supp. 2d 978, 990 (N.D. Cal. 2009). “[W]hile there is little authority on this issue
 3    within the Ninth Circuit, decisions from courts in other jurisdictions have made clear that
 4    dismissal of class allegations at the pleading stage should be done rarely and that the
 5    better course is to deny such a motion because the shape and form of a class action
 6    evolves only through the process of discovery.” In re Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. Wage &
 7    Hour Litig., 505 F. Supp. 2d 609, 615 (N.D. Cal. 2007) (internal quotation marks and
 8    citations omitted). However, “[s]ometimes the issues are plain enough from the pleadings
 9    to determine whether the interests of the absent parties are fairly encompassed within the
10    named plaintiff’s claim.” Gen. Tel. Co. of Sw. v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147, 160 (1982). The
11    Ninth Circuit has also held that “Rule 12(f) does not authorize district courts to strike
12    claims for damages on the ground that such claims are precluded as a matter of law.”
13    Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co., 618 F.3d 970, 975 (9th Cir. 2010).
14          The Court finds that the question of class suitability is more appropriately resolved
15    in a motion for class certification rather than a motion to strike. See Fernandez v.
16    CoreLogic Credco, LLC., 593 F. Supp. 3d 974, 996 (S.D. Cal. 2022); Rosales v. FitFlop
17    USA, LLC, 882 F. Supp. 2d 1168, 1179 (S.D. Cal. 2012); In re Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.
18    Wage & Hour Litig., 505 F. Supp. 2d at 615. Therefore, “so long as class action
19    allegations address each of the elements of Rule 23, relate to the subject matter of the
20    litigation, and are not redundant, immaterial, or impertinent, the court should find that the
21    allegations are sufficient to survive a motion to strike.” Rosales, 882 F. Supp. 2d at 1179
22    (internal quotation marks and citation omitted).
23          The Court finds Plaintiffs’ class action allegations are sufficient to survive a
24    motion to strike. The FAC alleges that the putative class action meets the four elements
25    of Rule 23(a)—numerosity, commonality, typicality, adequacy—as well as the alternative
26    requirements of Rule 23(b)(3)—predominance, superiority, and manageability. ECF
27    No. 10 ¶¶ 521–22; see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b). The class allegations also relate to the
28    subject matter of the litigation, and Defendants do not argue that they are redundant,

20                 H.     Class Period
21          Defendants contend that allegations regarding claims prior to September 18, 2019
22    should be struck because the statute of limitations is four years under section 17208 of
23    the California Business and Professions Code, and this matter was initiated on
24    September 18, 2023. ECF No. 35-1 at 30; see also ECF No. 1. Plaintiffs agree that the
25    applicable statute of limitations is four years, but argue that a class period beginning
26    October 13, 2016 is appropriate because “a pending class action tolls the limitations
27    period for absent class members” pursuant to American Pipe and Construction Co. v.
28    Utah, 414 U.S. 538 (1974). ECF No. 39 at 28–29: see also ECF No. 10 ¶ 522. Plaintiffs

                                                    18
                                                                                      23cv1729-LL-VET
     Case 3:23-cv-01729-LL-VET      Document 48 Filed 09/26/24        PageID.1734      Page 19
                                             of 21


 1    contend that in the pending class action matter of Zeff v. Greystar California, Inc.,
 2    No. 20-cv-07122-EMC (N.D. Cal. initiated Oct. 23, 2020), Zeff abandoned his security-
 3    deposit claims on June 22, 2023 when he moved for class certification only on his claim
 4    of unlawful late fees, which tolled the statute of limitations for absent class members who
 5    held security deposit claims against Greystar from October 13, 2016 until he abandoned
 6    the claims on June 22, 2023. ECF No. 39 at 28–29; see also ECF No. 10 ¶ 522 & n.28.
 7    Plaintiffs claim that when they filed this action on September 18, 2023, it again tolled the
 8    statute of limitations for those absent Zeff class members, “less the 84 days from when
 9    Zeff abandoned the claims.” ECF No. 39 at 29.
10          The Court finds the Motion to Strike the Plaintiffs’ proposed class period is not
11    premature. The arguments for and against this issue will not benefit from discovery and
12    resolving this discrete issue will further the purpose of “avoid[ing] the expenditure of
13    time and money that must arise from litigating spurious issues by dispensing with those
14    issues prior to trial.” Fantasy, Inc., 984 F.2d at 1527 (citation omitted).
15          The Court finds Plaintiffs’ proposed class period exceeds the statute of limitations
16    and American Pipe tolling is not applicable here. In American Pipe, the U.S. Supreme
17    Court held that “at least where class action status has been denied solely because of
18    failure to demonstrate that ‘the class is so numerous that joinder of all members is
19    impracticable,’ the commencement of the original class suit tolls the running of the
20    statute for all purported members of the class who make timely motions to intervene after
21    the court has found the suit inappropriate for class action status.” Am. Pipe & Const. Co.,
22    414 U.S. at 552–53. Subsequent courts have clarified that American Pipe tolls the statute
23    of limitations for members of the failed suit to allow them—as individual plaintiffs—to
24    intervene in or join an existing suit, or bring an individual suit after class certification is
25    denied. China Agritech v. Michael H. Resh, 584 U.S. 732, 735 (2018) (citation omitted).
26    In China Agritech, the Supreme Court revisited the American Pipe tolling rule and held
27    that upon denial of class certification, “a putative class member, in lieu of promptly
28    joining an existing suit or promptly filing an individual action,” may not “commence a

                                                    19
                                                                                      23cv1729-LL-VET
     Case 3:23-cv-01729-LL-VET      Document 48 Filed 09/26/24        PageID.1735      Page 20
                                             of 21


 1    class action anew beyond the time allowed by the applicable statute of limitations.” Id. at
 2    735–36. Therefore, American Pipe tolling applies to individual claims, not class claims,
 3    and it “does not permit a plaintiff who waits out the statute of limitations to piggyback on
 4    an earlier, timely filed class action.” Id. at 740. Plaintiffs seek to toll class claims to the
 5    earlier-filed Zeff class action, but that is not permitted. See id. (finding American Pipe
 6    permits tolling of individual claims, but not untimely successive class claims).
 7          Plaintiffs’ reliance on Falk v. Children’s Hospital Los Angeles, 237 Cal. App. 4th
 8    1454 (2015) to allow tolling for class claims is misplaced. See ECF No. 39 at 29. Falk
 9    was decided before China Agritech, and courts have recognized that Falk relied on cases
10    that have been abrogated by China Agritech. Fierro v. Landry’s Rest. Inc., 32 Cal. App.
11    5th 276, 297 (2019) (declining to follow Falk because “Falk relied principally on Yang
12    and Catholic Social Services to allow tolling of the statutes of limitations on the
13    otherwise untimely class claims; however, Yang and Catholic Social Services are no
14    longer good law.”). “Thus, upon denial of class certification in an action, a putative class
15    member may not commence the same class claim in a new action beyond the time
16    allowed by the limitation period applicable to the class claim.” Fierro, 32 Cal. App. 5th
17    at 297. The Court finds the only class claims that are timely are those that accrued within
18    the four-year statute of limitations period preceding the filing of Plaintiffs’ complaint on
19    September 18, 2023. See id.; see also China Agritech, 584 U.S. at 740. Accordingly, the
20    Court grants Defendants’ Motion to Strike the proposed class period with leave to amend.
