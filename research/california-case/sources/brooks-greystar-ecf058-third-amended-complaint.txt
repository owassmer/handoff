     I.     INTRODUCTION
 3           1.    Defendant GREYSTAR REAL ESTATE PARTNERS, LLC (collectively
 4   with Defendant GREYSTAR CALIFORNIA, INC., “GREYSTAR”) is the largest
 5   landlord in the United States. It publicly touts that it has “more than 822,100” residential
 6   units under management, many of which it also owns. GREYSTAR is estimated to have
 7   hundreds of thousands of residential units in California. Its business is extremely
 8   lucrative, making it one of the top real estate firms in the country in multiple categories,
 9   including apartment owners, developers, and builders.1
10           2.    To boost its already-massive profits, GREYSTAR has systematically
11   pilfered tenants’ deposits in violation of clear law, illegally turning security deposits into
12   a profit center.
13           3.    California has a detailed statutory scheme designed to protect tenants from
14   the common practice of landlords converting their deposits. Deposits are held in trust for
15   the benefit of the tenant and may only be used for purposes authorized by statute. Cal.
16   Civ. Code § 1950.5(b), (d). A landlord may only deduct from the deposits for unpaid
17   rent, repairs (other than ordinary wear and tear), necessary cleaning, and to replace or
18   restore personal property and appurtenances. Id., subd. (b).
19           4.    A critical component of the statutory scheme is the requirement that the
20   landlord substantiate any withholdings at the time a tenant moves out. The landlord must
21   provide detailed, mandatory disclosures, including an itemized statement “reasonably
22   describ[ing]” all work performed and “a copy of the bill, invoice, or receipt supplied by
23
     the person or entity performing the work.” Id., subd. (g). These requirements are

24
     designed to ensure that any charges are fully verifiable by the tenant.

25
             5.    GREYSTAR, however, uniformly fails to provide tenants with the

26
     disclosures mandated by statute. Among other things, it fails to provide sufficient

 6           479. The California Civil Code sets forth specific requirements for the handling
 7    of security deposits upon termination of a residential lease.
 8           480. Section 1950.5(b) provides that a landlord may only use a security deposit
 9    to satisfy charges against a former tenant only for: (1) rent in arrears; (2) repairs exclusive
10    of wear and tear; (3) cleaning required to bring the leasehold back to the condition it was
11    in when the tenant accepted the tenancy; and (4) “to remedy future defaults by the tenant
12    in any obligation under the rental agreement to restore, replace, or return personal
13    property or appurtenances” if provided for in the lease.
14           481. Civil Code section 1950.5(e) provides that “the landlord may claim on those
15    amounts as are reasonably necessary…” only for the specific categories identified in
16    section 1950.5(b).
17           482. Civil Code section 1950.5(g) provides that, to make deductions from a
18    security deposit more than $125.00, the deductions must be properly documented as
19    follows:
20                a. No later than 21 calendar days after the tenant vacates, the landlord “shall
21                   furnish the tenant, by personal delivery or by first-class mail, postage
22                   prepaid, a copy of an itemized statement indicating the basis for, and the
23
                     amount of, any security received and the disposition of the security, and shall

24
                     return any remaining portion of the security to the tenant.”
