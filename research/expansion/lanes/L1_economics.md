# L1 — Economics of the US rental operating system: where money, labor, time and failure concentrate, and where Handoff should expand

Lane: L1 (external, market economics). Date: 2026-09-28. Author: independent research lane. No internal company materials were read.

Conventions. Numbers carry a source id. "My arithmetic" means I combined cited inputs; the inputs are cited in the same sentence. `[unverified]` marks claims from model knowledge that I could not source. Company pages and investor posts are marketing claims, not measurements, and are labelled as such.

---

## 1. One-paragraph answer

The move-out is not a niche. It is the one moment in a tenancy where every party and every ledger meet: the departing resident's deposit and charges, the owner's money and approval, the vendors' quotes, work and invoices, the next resident's move-in date, and state deadlines. About 8.6 million renter households move each year (my arithmetic from 46.8M renter households and an 18.3% renter mover rate), and each move triggers roughly $1,000–$4,500 of make-ready spend plus 1–4 weeks of lost rent, and a deposit settlement worth about $5.7B a year in aggregate. The best expansion is therefore not "more of property management." It is to own the full **tenancy boundary**: the move-in condition baseline, the turn, the settlement, and recovery of what the former resident owes, sold to small and mid-sized third-party managers of single-family and small multifamily homes and priced against days of vacancy saved and dollars recovered. After that, the same machinery (scope, quote, owner approval, order, follow, pay, reconcile) extends to two higher-ticket, less crowded events: seasonal mass turns (student housing, which fits Charlottesville) and owner-funded projects (renovation, casualty repair). Do not expand into routine maintenance tickets, leasing chat, deposit-alternative insurance, or institutional owners now: those are crowded, regulated, or insourcing. Two warnings shape everything: the move-out/deposit space filled with AI entrants in 2026, and a small property manager's profit is about $10 per unit per month, so Handoff's price must be paid from owner savings or recovered tenant charges, not from the manager's margin. The question is also early: with no paying customer, the most valuable next step is proving that the first product saves vacancy days and recovers money at one operator, because that single number decides which expansion is worth it.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 The stock, the owners and who manages them

- The US had 46.8 million renter-occupied units in Q2 2026, 31.3% of all housing units, with a 7.3% rental vacancy rate.[9]
- The 2021 Rental Housing Finance Survey counted 49.5 million rental units in 19.3 million properties; 46% of units sit in 1–4 unit properties, and 86% of rental properties contain a single unit.[1]
- Individual investors own about 70% of rental properties and 38% of rental units.[1]
- Only about 22% of small (1–4 unit) rental properties are professionally managed, versus 84% of properties with 150+ units.[1] Applying 22% to the ~22.8M units in 1–4 unit properties suggests on the order of 17–18M small-property units are self-managed (my arithmetic from [1]; this mixes a property share with a unit count, so treat it as rough).
- The 2024 RHFS (released February 2026) shows individuals still own 59.6% of single-family rental properties, down from 70.9% in 2021, with LLCs at 20.6% and REITs/corporations at 1.8%.[8] I read this from a trade summary of the survey, not the Census tables themselves.
- Institutional investors own 4% (Seattle) to 22% (Jacksonville) of single-family rentals in the six metros GAO studied.[7]
- Small multifamily (5–49 units) is about 17% of rental housing: 8.2 million units in nearly 500,000 properties.[35]
- Mom-and-pop landlords (10 or fewer properties) own more than a third of US rental housing.[36]
- New in 2026: the ROAD to Housing Act became law on July 11, 2026.[32]
  - From January 7, 2027, entities controlling 350+ single-family homes may not buy more, with exceptions for build-to-rent and renovate-to-rent; there is no forced divestiture.[33][45]
  - The law also loosens voucher (HCV) initial inspections, allowing families to move in before a PHA inspection.[45]

**Implication.** The unit count is split between two very different worlds. Large multifamily is professionally managed and runs on software. Single-family and small multifamily units are mostly owned by individuals and mostly self-managed. The third-party manager of single-family and small multifamily homes sits between them, and that is where a turnover service fits best.

### 2.2 The size of the flows per unit per year

Professionally managed apartments (NAA Income/Expense IQ 2024, ~1,000 same-store properties):[6]
- Income: $21,017 per unit.
- Total operating expenses: $8,657 per unit.
- Administrative and payroll: $2,323 per unit.
- Taxes and insurance: $2,998, of which insurance was $777, up 10.8% after a 25% rise in 2023.
- Utilities: $1,304.
- Repairs and maintenance: $1,098, up 28% since 2021.
- Vacancy and rent loss: $1,323.
- Leasing: $292, with turnover costs up 17.5% year over year.
- Bad debt: $75.

Single-family rental at scale (Invitation Homes, FY2025, 76,819 same-store homes):[4]
- Turnover rate: 22.8%. Bad debt: 0.7% of gross rental revenue.
- Repairs and maintenance opex: $100.4M, about $1,307 per home. Turn opex net of resident recoveries: $39.7M.
- Total "cost to maintain" including capex: 917 + 1,153 + 959 + 802 = $3,831 per home for the year (my arithmetic from quarterly figures in [4]).
- Turn opex plus turn capex: about $78.6M. Across about 17,500 turns, that is about $4,480 per turn, net of resident recoveries (my arithmetic from [4]).

All rental properties (RHFS 2021): median operating expense $380 per unit per month; median capital improvement spend $840 per unit per year.[1]

Aggregate pools (my arithmetic, rough orders of magnitude):
- Repairs and maintenance: 46.8M units × $1,098–$1,307 ≈ $51–61B a year.[9][6][4]
- Bad debt: 46.8M × $75 ≈ $3.5B a year. An older NAA figure of $92 per unit lost to collections gives about $4.3B.[9][6][12]

### 2.3 The move-out event: frequency, cost, time

**Frequency**
- Renter household mobility was 18.3% in 2024, down from 37.2% in 1981. It was 38% for renter householders aged 18–24 and 7.8% for those 65+.[48] Applied to 46.8M renter households, that is about 8.6M moves a year (my arithmetic from [48][9]).
- Operator-reported turnover is higher, because it is measured per expiring lease: 40.2% at MAA in 2025, which MAA calls historically low.[3] It was 46.8% in NAA's 2018 survey data.[11] Single-family runs lower, at 22.8% for Invitation Homes.[4]

**Direct cost per turn**
- NAA's guidance uses a "conservatively calculated" $1,800 per move-out.[11]
- One tech-enabled single-family manager reports a $957 average direct cost across 232 Bay Area turns, with 11 days to ready. This is company data published as marketing.[14]
- Invitation Homes spends about $4,480 per turn including capex (my arithmetic from [4]).
- Vendor and marketing figures of $4,200–$6,800 "total churn cost" include lost rent and leasing, and are not measurements.[15]

**Time**
- NAA says operators use seven working days as a typical make-ready guideline, with targets of three to five.[11]
- One vacant day at MAA's $1,687 average rent is about $55 (my arithmetic from [3]). Seven to 25 vacant days is therefore about $390–$1,390 per turn.

**Aggregate:** about 8.6M turns × $957–$4,480 ≈ $8–38B a year of direct turn spend. Lost rent adds about $3–12B at 7–25 days (my arithmetic from [48][9][3]). The ranges are wide. That width is itself a finding: nobody publishes a clean national turn-cost series.

**Where the time goes: coordination, not work**
- In an NAA case study of a 300-unit Dallas property, staff spent 15 hours a week in peak season, and 10 hours a week otherwise, on "contractor management (emails, text messages, phone calls, meetings, access to homes, etc.)". That cost about $16,200 a year in labor. Cutting five vacant days per turn was worth $45,000 on 180 turns.[11]
- Practitioners on BiggerPockets in 2026 named the gap between inspection and scope, and waiting for owner approval, as the main sources of delay. They cited standing repair limits as the fix.[42] One wrote: "The root issue is rarely just cost. It's coordination."[43]
- In NAA/AppFolio's 2025 survey (n=1,984), "move-outs/unit turns" had one of the lowest shares of operators "completely confident" that best practices are always followed: 48%. About half of operators find profit-harming errors daily or weekly.[13]

### 2.4 The departing resident's money: deposits, charges, disputes, recovery

**Deposits**
- 83% of renters pay a security deposit. The median reached $795 in 2025.[2]
- That implies roughly $31B of deposits held at any time, and about $5.7B settled each year at move-out (my arithmetic from [2][9][48]).

**Law and enforcement**
- New York caps deposits at one month's rent. It requires return or an itemized statement within 14 days, and gives tenants a right to a pre-move-out inspection with a chance to cure defects.[25]
- Virginia caps deposits at two months' rent and requires itemization within 45 days.[26] Virginia extended the pay-or-quit period from 5 days to 14 days, effective July 1, 2026.[49]
- The FTC is now in the space. Invitation Homes paid $48M in redress, partly for "unfairly withholding security deposit deductions."[2] Greystar agreed to pay $24M over fee disclosure (December 2025).[46] The FTC has also issued an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on rental housing fees. The copy I read is an undated pre-publication version citing orders from December 2025.[2]

**Disputes**
- In a 2021 survey, property managers estimated that 40% of renters challenge their deposit refund. Walls and paint (56%) and flooring (39%) were the top deductions. 52% of renters took no move-in photos.[16] This is an old, vendor-run survey. I found no better national measure of dispute rates. That is a gap.

**Recovery**
- NAA puts collection-agency recovery of former-resident debt at 15–20%, sometimes under 10% in affordable housing. Agencies charge 20–40% of what they collect.[12]
- NAA advises sending balances to collections within 30 days of move-out. Broken-lease balances often exceed $5,000.[12]
- Evictions: roughly 1 filing per 17 renter households a year, and 1 in 40 households evicted (2000–2016 data).[10]

**Deposit alternatives**
- Deposit alternatives (surety, insurance, fee products) charge non-refundable fees, typically $10–$30 a month.[47] Consumer advocates say millions of renters now use them, and that landlords use them to avoid deposit law.[44] Expect this category to be regulated.

**Implication.** The settlement step moves real money under hard deadlines and rising enforcement, while evidence quality is poor (no move-in photos, weak documentation). Recovery after the fact is inefficient: 15–20% recovered, minus a 20–40% fee. Every dollar settled correctly at move-out is worth several dollars chased later.

### 2.5 Labor and the manager's economics

- NARPM's benchmark of 50+ residential property management firms, taken from their books: average adjusted profit margin 6% (top 12 firms: 25%), revenue about $1,809 per unit per year, profit about $10 per unit per month, and 25% annual "unit churn" (owners leaving). This is 2017 data, published 2019.[19]
- Owners (Buildium 2026 survey, n=300 small owners):[29][27]
  - 23% say their manager delivers excellent value.
  - 46% name maintenance as their top stressor, and 59% name it as the most valuable service.
  - 84% want to approve large repairs.
  - Poor communication is the top reason owners switch managers (57%).
  - 56% hired a manager mainly for maintenance support.
- Buildium's industry survey says AI adoption rose from 20% to 58% in a year, but only 8% of firms fully automate any process.[27] AppFolio's 2026 survey says 78% cannot rely on the AI in their legacy software, and 45% plan to consolidate their tools.[28]
- For larger multifamily operators (250+ units, n=630), Zego reports 95 manual hours a month on routine tasks, costing about $43,000 a year.[30]

**Implication.** A small manager cannot pay for software out of margin. Priced at $150–$400 per turn, at 30% turnover, a turn product costs $45–$120 per unit per year: 2.5–6.6% of the manager's revenue and up to its entire profit (my arithmetic from [19]). It must be billed through to the owner, or funded by vacancy days saved or tenant charges recovered. The owner is the budget holder, and the owner's pain (maintenance surprises, slow answers) is exactly what a turn product addresses.

### 2.6 Where the software money is actually made

- AppFolio earned $950.8M in FY2025 from 9.4M units, about $101 per unit.[5] Of that, $721.5M (76%) came from "Value Added Services" (payments, screening, risk mitigation/insurance, maintenance), and only $211.5M from subscriptions.[5]
- A maintenance vendor charges property managers $3.99–$9.99 per unit per month, and pitches "$30/unit per month" of revenue the manager can generate from residents.[56]

**Implication.** In this market, money is made on the flows (payments, insurance, fees charged to residents), not on software seats. A company that pays vendors and settles deposits is standing in those flows. That also brings regulatory load: trust accounting, money transmission, and fee rules.

### 2.7 Other parties

- **Insurers.** Multifamily premiums rose 14%, 22% and 45% in successive years to 2024 in one Fed survey (35 owners, a convenience sample).[17] NMHC saw the first property-rate decline since 2017, while liability lines kept rising.[18] Only 48% of renters carry renters insurance, and 32% say their landlord requires it.[51] Insurance is a cost the owner bears; the lever is claim prevention and documentation, not distribution.
- **Lenders.** Fannie Mae requires lenders to ensure borrowers hold replacement reserves for capital replacement and major maintenance.[52] The RHFS finds 41% of rental properties carry a mortgage.[1] Lenders care about condition and reserves, not about turns. I found no evidence that lenders pay for turn operations.
- **Municipalities.** Proactive rental inspection and registration programs are spreading. Some cities require owners to hire licensed third-party inspectors.[38] State commissions spot-audit managers' trust accounts. In one NC case it was "impossible to determine exactly how much of the money in the trust account belonged to which owner."[41]
- **Vendors.** There are about 122,600 plumbing/HVAC establishments (1.29M employees).[37] Outsourced networks advertise 30,000 vendors across 55 trades.[22] Large single-family operators rely on 1099 contractors, not employees.[43] I found no rigorous source on vendor payment terms in property management. That is a gap.
- **Public housing agencies.** HUD finds landlords avoid vouchers because of damage and deposit risk and inspection bureaucracy. PHAs pay vacancy-loss and damage incentives, for example up to 2 months of vacancy payments in San Diego.[24]

### 2.8 Competition moved into this exact space in 2025–2026

- Obligo launched an "autonomous AI agent" for deposits in June 2026. It said "Move-out is where the Deposit Agent can create the most immediate value."[53]
- TurnOps sells AI move-out damage assessment, disposition letters and deadline tracking.[54] Frontdesk sells an AI move-out assistant covering notice through "turn-vendor coordination, and final ledger close," integrated with the major PMS platforms.[55]
- Vendoroo claims 500+ manager customers for AI maintenance coordination.[57] Uniti raised a $12M Series A in July 2026 for agentic property operations.[21]
- EliseAI claims coverage of more than 10% of US apartments and more than $100M ARR. Its investor says it handles maintenance, billing and delinquencies.[20]
- A BiggerPockets manager already runs move-out inspection photos through an AI model to build same-day make-ready estimates and split owner charges from tenant charges.[42]

**Implication.** "AI for move-out" is no longer a differentiator. What is still rare is accountability: taking responsibility for the whole turn, spending the owner's money within instructions, and being judged on days-to-ready and dollars recovered.

### 2.9 Institutional shifts

- Institutional single-family owners are insourcing management. KKR reportedly moved about 10,000 homes away from its asset manager; one manager lost about 30% of its doors. This is a practitioner newsletter citing two unnamed people.[31]
- Tech-enabled managers are consolidating: Evernest reached about 23,000 units across 50+ markets after its Poplar acquisition in 2025.[23]
- The ROAD Act caps acquisition growth by large single-family investors, except build-to-rent and renovate-to-rent.[33]

### 2.10 Founder geographies

- **Charlottesville.** Soft rental market: 8.7% vacancy, versus 5.5% in 2020. Student households are about 14% of renter households (26,685 UVA students in fall 2025).[50] Virginia's 2026 tenant laws raise process requirements.[49][26]
- **Student housing everywhere.** More than 60% of residents turn in a 2–3 week window. Operators must contract out.[40]
- **New York.** Strict deposit rules: 14-day itemization, one-month cap, pre-move-out inspection.[25] The FARE Act (June 2025) shifted broker fees onto landlords, raising leasing cost per turn.[25]

## 3. Expansion options (my taxonomy)

I group options by what grows: **(A) the event** the company owns, **(B) the customer**, **(C) the party served**, and **(D) the business model**. Confidence reflects evidence strength, not how attractive the option is.

### A. Grow the event

**A1. Own the tenancy boundary (move-in baseline + turn + settlement + recovery)**
- **What.** Add a move-in condition record, pre-move-out walk and cure notice, and deadline-driven settlement. Carry former-resident balances through payment plans and hand-off to collections. Close the owner ledger.
- **Who pays.** The owner, billed through the manager as a per-turn fee. Optionally, a success fee on recovered tenant charges.
- **Why these founders.** The product already runs the turn. Settlement is the next step on the same records. Owen's quantitative bent suits pricing on measured days saved and dollars recovered.
- **Evidence.**
  - $5.7B of deposits settled a year (derived from [2][9][48]).
  - Deadlines of 14 and 45 days.[25][26]
  - 40% dispute estimate.[16]
  - 15–20% agency recovery.[12]
  - FTC enforcement.[2][46]
  - Coordination delays.[11][42]
- **Risks.** Crowded: Obligo, TurnOps and Frontdesk all sell move-out AI.[53][54][55]
  - PMS vendors could bundle it.[5]
  - Consumer-protection exposure if charges are aggressive. Collecting on the owner's behalf can trigger debt-collection rules [unverified]. PMS platforms may bundle it.
- **What would falsify it.** Pilots show under 2 vacant days saved per turn and no measurable lift in recovered charges. Or managers will not pass a per-turn fee to owners.
- **Confidence.** Medium-high on need, medium on winnability.

**A2. Mass-turn surge service (student and seasonal turns)**
- **What.** Plan, scope and staff hundreds of turns inside a 2–3 week window. Pre-book vendors, pre-scope before move-out, and run daily readiness.
- **Who pays.** Student-housing owners and managers, and scattered-site student landlords. Priced per bed or per unit.
- **Why these founders.** Charlottesville (UVA) gives a local test market and Connor's introductions. Capacity, not software, is the bottleneck, and Handoff's agent absorbs coordination load.
- **Evidence.** More than 60% of students turn at once; operators must contract out.[40] Students are 14% of Charlottesville renter households.[50]
- **Risks.** Seasonal revenue. Purpose-built student operators have in-house turn teams [unverified]. Vendor scarcity in August is physical and software cannot fix it.
- **What would falsify it.** Student operators already hit move-in dates with slack. Or scattered-site student landlords will not pay.
- **Confidence.** Medium-low. Thin national evidence, strong local fit.

**A3. Owner-funded projects: renovation, capex, casualty repair**
- **What.** The same scope, quote, approve, order, follow and pay loop for bigger jobs: water-damage repair, roof/HVAC replacement, renovate-to-rent rehabs.
- **Who pays.** Owners, including investors using the ROAD Act renovate-to-rent path. Priced as a percentage of project cost or a fixed fee.
- **Why these founders.** Same machinery. Higher ticket per event. Fewer AI competitors in this space.
- **Evidence.**
  - Median capex is $840 per unit per year.[1]
  - Invitation Homes' cost to maintain, including capex, is $3,831 per home.[4]
  - 84% of owners want to approve large repairs.[29]
  - Renovate-to-rent is an exception under the new law.[33]
  - Lenders require replacement reserves.[52]
- **Risks.** Lumpy demand. Projects need construction judgment, and a general contractor's liability may attach. Insurance claims add adjuster complexity.
- **What would falsify it.** Owners prefer a trusted general contractor. Or project volume per customer is too low to learn from.
- **Confidence.** Medium-low. Sparse direct evidence.

**A4. Routine maintenance tickets**
- **What.** 24/7 intake, triage and dispatch.
- **Evidence.** Work-order spend of about $235 per order and repair speed of about 7 days.[15] Maintenance is owners' main stressor.[29]
- **Assessment.** The most crowded category: Vendoroo, Latchel and EliseAI all sell here.[57][56][20]
  - AppFolio ships its own AI maintenance intake and triage.[5] HappyCo is shipping maintenance AI too [unverified].
- **Confidence.** Low as a place to win. Useful only as a feature of A1.

### B. Grow the customer

**B1. Small and mid-sized third-party managers of single-family and small multifamily homes (100–3,000 doors)**
- **Why.** The core buyer. They lack onsite staff, use 1099 vendors, and face owner churn over maintenance and communication.[19][29][43]
- **Risks.** Thin margins (6%). Buying runs through the PMS ecosystem.
- **Confidence.** Medium-high.

**B2. New York small-building owners and managers**
- **Why.** The strictest deposit rules make settlement discipline worth more.[25] Founder access.
- **Risks.** Rent-stabilized complexity and housing-court culture.
- **Confidence.** Medium.

**B3. Self-managing individual landlords (about 17–18M small-property units, rough)**
- **Why.** Large count and no manager.[1]
- **Risks.** Low willingness to pay and high acquisition cost. This is effectively becoming the property manager.
- **Confidence.** Low.

**B4. Institutional single-family and large multifamily owners**
- **Why not now.** Insourcing, the acquisition freeze, and well-funded AI vendors.[31][33][20]
  - Large operators already run turns at scale.[4]
- **Confidence.** Low.

**B5. Public housing agencies and voucher landlords**
- **Why.** Damage and deposit risk and inspection friction keep landlords out; PHAs pay incentives.[24] ROAD Act inspection changes.[45]
- **Risks.** Slow public procurement.
- **Confidence.** Low. Worth watching.

### C. Grow the parties served

**C1. Departing-resident settlement channel**
- **What.** A resident-facing, evidence-based statement, dispute and payment-plan flow.
- **Who pays.** The owner, through higher recovery, and fewer disputes and complaints.
- **Evidence.** Low agency recovery rates and high fees.[12] Enforcement.[2]
- **Risk.** Regulated collection activity [unverified].
- **Confidence.** Medium, as part of A1.

**C2. Vendor back office (quotes, scheduling, invoicing, fast pay)**
- **What.** Become the trades' preferred channel into rental work.
- **Who pays.** Vendors (fast-pay fee) or payment take rate.
- **Evidence.** Money is made on flows (76% of AppFolio revenue).[5] About 122,600 HVAC/plumbing establishments.[37]
- **Risks.** Two-sided cold start. Money-transmission and trust-account rules.[41]
- **Confidence.** Low now, higher later.

**C3. Owner transparency and approval layer**
- **Evidence.** Owners want approval rights, fast answers and no surprises.[29]
- **Assessment.** Build it into A1. It is weak as a standalone product.
- **Confidence.** Medium as a feature.

### D. Change the business model

**D1. Per-turn outcome pricing with a days-to-ready guarantee**
- **What.** Charge per turn; refund part of the fee if the unit is not ready by date X.
- **Why.** Ties price to the owner's $55+ per vacant day (my arithmetic from [3]). Differentiates from AI-feature sellers.
- **Risk.** Absorbing vendor delays.
- **Confidence.** Medium.

**D2. Success fee on recovered tenant charges**
- **Why.** Aligned with owner loss. Could substitute for collection agencies charging 20–40%.[12]
- **Risk.** Incentive to over-charge residents; the FTC is watching.[2]
- **Confidence.** Medium-low.

**D3. Tech-enabled turn contractor (hold vendor margin, sell a fixed-price turn)**
- **Why.** Revenue per event rises from about $200 of software fees to $1,000–$4,500 of turn gross merchandise value (GMV).[14][4]
  - Precedents exist: Lessen, Valet Quick Turns.[22][11]
- **Risks.** Services margins, workmanship liability, working capital.
- **Confidence.** Medium-low.

**D4. Roll-up: become or acquire a property manager**
- **Why.** AI could lift the 6% margin toward the 25% of top performers.[19] Consolidation is under way.[23]
- **Risks.** Capital, 25% owner churn, and an operating identity change.[19]
- **Confidence.** Low without capital.

**D5. Deposit-alternative or insurance product**
- **Why not.** Rising advocacy and regulatory pushback; heavy capital and licensing needs.[44][47]
- **Confidence.** Low. Avoid.

**D6. Embedded distribution through PMS marketplaces**
- **What.** Distribute through AppFolio, Buildium or Rent Manager integrations.
- **Why.** Reach.
- **Risk.** The platforms monetize the same flows and ship their own AI.[5][28]
- **Confidence.** Medium as a channel, low as a strategy.

## 4. Ranking and reasoning

1. **A1 + B1 + D1: own the tenancy boundary for small and mid-sized single-family/small-multifamily managers, priced per turn against days saved.** This is where money (turn spend and deposit settlement), time (vacancy days) and failure (disputes, missed deadlines, write-offs) all concentrate in one event. It also uses the product that exists. The manager can sell it to owners because it attacks their top stressor and their main reason for switching managers.[29]
   Win on accountability and measured outcomes, not on AI features, which competitors now ship.[53][54][55]
2. **A2: mass turns in Charlottesville.** A cheap, local, time-boxed test of whether Handoff can run many turns in parallel. The window gives a clear pass/fail each August. Rank it second because it is a proof of capacity, and capacity is what an agent should be best at.
3. **A3: owner-funded projects and casualty repair.** Same loop, higher ticket, less competition. Enter once A1 shows reliable vendor execution.
4. **C1/D2: resident settlement and recovery,** as the money-making tail of A1. Needs a legal read on debt-collection rules before charging a success fee.
5. **B2: New York,** as the second geography, because its strict deposit rules raise the value of A1.
6. **C2: vendor network and payments.** This is the long-run moat and margin (money is made on flows), but it only works after turn volume exists.

Deprioritized: routine maintenance (A4), self-managing landlords (B3), institutions (B4), deposit alternatives (D5), a roll-up (D4) unless capital appears.

**Reasoning in one line.** The expansion path should follow the money that already passes through Handoff's hands (owner funds to vendors, resident funds to or from the owner), toward bigger or more concentrated events. It should not follow feature adjacency into crowded front-office AI.

## 5. Challenges to the premise

- **Expansion is premature.** There is no paying customer yet. The market data cannot answer the key unknowns: vacancy days saved per turn, and extra dollars recovered per move-out. Expansion choice should wait on one operator's numbers.
- **"Build move-out, then expand" may be backwards.** Settlement quality depends on move-in evidence (52% of renters took no move-in photos).[16] The tenancy boundary is a loop, and the "expansion" may need to happen first: capture move-in condition from day one of a customer relationship.
- **The move-out/deposit category is crowding fast.**[53][54][55]
  - If the wedge is copied inside PMS platforms,[5] expansion is a race. Speed of proof matters more than breadth.
- **Soft rents raise the stakes but tighten budgets.** Rents for professionally managed apartments fell 0.6% year over year in Q4 2025, and 22.7M renter households were cost-burdened in 2024.[34] Owners will want turn savings, but departing residents will have less capacity to pay charges, so recovery upside may be smaller than hoped.
- **Turn frequency is declining.** Renter mobility has fallen from 37.2% to 18.3%, and MAA calls 40.2% turnover historically low.[48][3] Per-turn cost is rising,[6] so value per event grows while event count shrinks. A per-unit subscription may age better than a per-turn fee. Test both.
- **Misaligned incentives in the buyer.** NARPM notes that managers earn maintenance revenue from owner coordination fees and vendor referral income.[19] A product that cuts owner turn cost may cut manager revenue. The buyer and the beneficiary are different people.
- **Platform cost.** Pricing at $4–$10 per unit per month (the going rate for maintenance tools[56]) may not cover a Foundry-based production stack. This depends on the undecided production platform [unverified].
- **Maybe Handoff is a services company.** The strongest economics might come from holding the turn contract (D3), not selling software. That is a different company to run.

## 6. Founder-fact assumptions my conclusions depend on

- **Capital.** Ranking assumes modest capital. With significant capital, D3 (turn contractor) or D4 (roll-up) rise.
- **Full-time status.** A2 (August surge) and a live pilot need at least one founder available for operations in season.
- **Charlottesville access.** Assumes Connor's introductions include student landlords or single-family/small-multifamily managers. If they are only large multifamily operators, A2 and B1 weaken locally.
- **New York access.** B2 assumes access to small-building owners or managers, not just renter experience.
- **Property-management operating experience.** None is assumed. A3 and D3 need construction and trade judgment the founders may lack.
- **Network size.** Assumes introductions number in the tens, not hundreds. That favors one deep pilot over a broad launch.

---

## 7. Questions only private operator data can answer

1. Days from move-out to rent-ready, split by stage: access, inspection, scope, approval, vendor start, completion, final QC.
2. Turn cost per unit: vendor invoices versus internal labor, and the share billed to the resident versus the owner.
3. Deposit outcomes: percent returned in full, average deduction, dispute rate, deadline misses, and any penalties paid.
4. Former-resident balances: amount at move-out, recovered within 30/90/365 days, placed with agencies, net recovery after fees.
5. Owner approval latency, and standing repair limits per owner.
6. Manager revenue from maintenance coordination fees and vendor markups (the incentive question).
7. Vendor behavior: quote response time, no-show rate, payment terms, and whether faster pay improves priority.
8. How much a manager could raise owner fees, or charge a per-turn fee, without losing doors.
9. Seasonality: the share of annual turns falling in the peak 6 weeks (student and non-student).
10. Software stack and switching costs (which PMS, what integrations are allowed).

## 8. Coverage gaps

- 2024 RHFS detail tables (management by size, expenses) were not read directly. The Census API needs a key, so the 2024 figures come via a trade summary.[8]
- AHS was not used. BLS occupational data was blocked. The AMH 10-K on EDGAR was blocked (the press release was used instead).[39] Zillow research pages were blocked.
- No rigorous national data on deposit dispute rates, vendor payment terms, days-to-ready for small managers, or the count of third-party management firms. IBISWorld is paywalled.
- Reddit was not searched; practitioner sentiment comes from BiggerPockets only.[42][43]
- Lender-side (DSCR/small-investor loans) and landlord insurance for single-family rentals are thinly covered.
- Several benchmark figures are company marketing.[14][15][20]
- Several competitor claims are also marketing.[54][56][57]
