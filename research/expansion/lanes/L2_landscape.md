# L2 — Landscape and outcomes: rental operations companies 2015–2026, and what they imply for Handoff's expansion

Lane: external market landscape. Written 2026-09-28. I did not read any internal company material. Every outside fact carries a ledger citation. Claims from my own background knowledge are marked [unverified]. Numbers from vendors' marketing pages are labelled as claims, not measurements.

## 1. Answer in one paragraph

Across 2015–2026, the pattern is consistent. Software that sits on top of the property management system (PMS) either gets bundled by the PMS vendor or sold to one. Companies that took on balance-sheet risk (master leases, rent guarantees, homes bought to rent) mostly failed, were merged, or sold below their peak. Firms that did well captured a money flow: payments, insurance, deposits, recovery, or vendor spend. EliseAI is the exception, and it grew by adding workflows for large operators, not by changing segment. Since mid-2025 the incumbents have shipped AI agents for leasing, maintenance, payments and collections. In June 2026 Obligo launched a deposit agent embedded in Yardi, RealPage, AppFolio and Buildium. So Handoff's next step, the tenant-account settlement, is already contested. Turn coordination is also filling up with small AI tools. My recommendation: do not expand by adding more features beside the PMS. Instead: (a) pick the segment where turns depend most on outside vendors and email and least on on-site staff (scattered-site single-family and small multifamily managed for third-party owners); (b) widen from move-out to every episodic, vendor-heavy, owner-funded project for that segment; (c) charge on outcomes or on the money that passes through, not per seat. Owning or federating a management company, as in the 2026 AI roll-ups, is the high-variance "different shape". It only makes sense once the software shows that each staff member can run many more units, and only if capital exists. Confidence: medium on the diagnosis, low-to-medium on the ranking, because I have no operator data.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Incumbents own the system of record and are moving into agents

- AppFolio made $951 million of revenue in fiscal 2025, up 20%, and managed 9.4 million units.[16] Of that revenue, $721.5 million came from "Value Added Services" (payments, screening, insurance and similar) against $211.5 million from core subscriptions.[16] So about 76% of revenue is transactional (my calculation from [16]).
- In June 2025 AppFolio launched "Realm-X Performers", agents for leasing and maintenance that diagnose requests, create work orders and log summaries.[15] In October 2024 it bought LiveEasy, a moving-concierge company, to extend into the resident's move-in journey.[17]
- Entrata's S-1 (May 2026) reports $509.3 million revenue in 2025, up from $412.0 million.[25] It powers 2.5 million units, which it calls roughly 10% of US multifamily.[25] Its paid AI suite covers Leasing, Payments, Renewals and Maintenance AI.[25] Q1 2026 revenue was $143.5 million with $23.3 million of net income.[26]
- Entrata bought Colleen AI in June 2024 for AI collections.[24] Colleen had launched a "post-resident recovery" service in December 2023 to collect from former residents.[47] In June 2025 Entrata added maintenance AI and "Make Ready boards" for unit turns.[23]
- RealPage's Lumina AI suite lists agents for leasing, resident, facilities, finance, operations and spend, next to deposit alternatives, vendor marketplace and vendor credentialing products.[28] In November 2025 the DOJ settled with RealPage, which has to stop using nonpublic competitor data to set rents.[27]
- Yardi launched Virtuoso AI agents in September 2025, with a "Virtuoso Marketplace" of pre-built agents made by independent consultants and industry experts.[21] It also launched connectors that let Anthropic's Claude read live Yardi data.[22]

Implication: any capability that is one step from the PMS ledger will be bundled. This includes leasing chat, maintenance intake, collections nudges and make-ready boards. Most incumbent revenue comes from money flows, not software seats.

### 2.2 Getting access to the PMS is a gate, and each vendor sets it differently

- Yardi charges "an annual license fee per interface". To qualify for the Standard Interface Partnership, a company must be "two years old and have at least three active Voyager clients".[20]
- AppFolio's API is limited by plan tier: none on Core, read-only on Plus, read and write on Max, plus access for approved Stack partners.[19] This comes from a third-party integrator's 2026 write-up, not from AppFolio, so treat it as a claim. AppFolio's partner page advertises reach to "over 20 thousand AppFolio customers and over 8 million units".[18] Point solutions such as Rently (December 2025) and Flex (October 2025) use the Stack marketplace for distribution.[80][81]
- Rentvine, a newer PMS for single-family and small multifamily managers, raised $74 million from Mainsail in 2024 and advertises an open REST API.[40]
- Obligo shows the payoff of patience. It is now embedded in Yardi, RealPage, AppFolio and Buildium.[11]

Implication: an agent that works mainly by email does not depend much on a single PMS API. That is a real structural advantage for Handoff. Deterministic money checks still need ledger data, so the first PMS integration matters. Yardi enterprise accounts are gated for a new company. AppFolio-tier and Rentvine customers are easier to reach.

### 2.3 Maintenance and turn coordination: crowded at both ends

- Established players: Property Meld reported more than 650 customers and 450,000 units at its Series B,[6] and bought the AI intake company Mezo in January 2025.[5] Lula raised a $28 million Series A in February 2025; it processed more than 100,000 work orders for more than 125 property managers in 2024 and runs a contractor network.[39] HappyCo claims more than 5.5 million units and launched "Joy AI" on maintenance records in 2025.[14] RentCheck sells side-by-side move-in/move-out photo comparisons for deductions.[13]
- Latchel now calls itself an "AI front office for growing property managers".[4] It has moved from maintenance to calls in general. Vendoroo sells an AI maintenance coordinator with a human expert behind it.[75]
- Services at scale: Lessen bought SMS Assist for $950 million. Its CEO said SMS had about $700 million of revenue that he believed was "under-monetized", and he described the combined company as a two-sided marketplace with landlords and vendors.[3] Lessen sells "Turns and Make Readies" to single-family operators.[76]
- A 2026 long tail of turn-specific AI tools has appeared. TurnOps documents damage with AI and generates dispositions.[63] Market Ready Turn says it "runs a unit from move-out inspection to re-listed".[64] TurnBoard lets vendors accept dispatch by text.[65] APTS Turn Pro sells video move-out inspections.[66] Frontdesk's AI move-out assistant goes from notice to final ledger across the major PMSs.[67] Two publish recovered-dollar figures: TurnOps claims $2,400 per unit[63] and APTS claims $1,150 per turn.[66] These are marketing claims, not measurements.

Implication: "AI that coordinates a turn" is no longer rare. What stays scarce is a service that takes responsibility for the result: it spends the owner's money within rules, pays the vendors, and answers for the outcome. Almost everyone above sells a tool or a network, not accountability.

### 2.4 The settlement step is contested by money-flow companies

- Deposit replacement is funded and mature. LeaseLock reports $14 billion of leases insured across more than 2 million leases (July 2025).[9] Obligo raised $35 million in October 2024[10] and powers AppFolio's deposit options.[12] Rhino raised $95 million at a $500 million valuation in 2021, then laid off 57 people (over 20% of staff) in February 2022 to reach profitability sooner.[8]
- In June 2026 Obligo launched the "Deposit Agent", described as "the first autonomous AI agent purpose-built for lease security and deposit administration". It covers charges, refunds, compliance and dispute prevention, and it is sold through the PMSs where Obligo is already embedded.[11]
- Roost handles cash deposits, refund automation, itemized statements and balance recovery.[61][62] Pay Ready sells post-resident recovery.[60][89] Zego added automated deposit refund payouts in June 2024.[71] DepositHive targets independent landlords: segregated interest-bearing deposit accounts, state deadline tracking and itemized statements.[68]
- Recovery economics are poor. NAA puts typical collection-agency recovery at 15–20% and says documentation quality raises recovery.[70] Colleen claims the chance of collecting drops 16% for every 30 days of delinquency.[47] That is a vendor claim.
- Renters' experience: in a TurboTenant survey (2024), 91% of renters paid a deposit, the average was $2,011, and only 42% got it all back.[69] This is survey evidence, not audited data.

Implication: a settlement module on its own competes with embedded, insurer-backed firms that already control the deposit. Handoff's edge is joining settlement to the turn it just ran. It already holds the vendor quotes, invoices, photos and owner instructions that justify each charge. That link, not settlement alone, is the defensible part.

### 2.5 Regulation is making move-out a documentation-heavy, rules-based process

- California AB 2801 requires photos at the start of tenancies beginning on or after July 1, 2025. It bars charging for professional cleaning unless it is needed to restore the unit's original condition, and it bars deductions for items missing from the itemized statement after an initial inspection.[48]
- Virginia requires an itemized written disposition within 45 days, a notice of the tenant's right to attend the move-out inspection, and an inspection within 72 hours of possession.[50]
- New York caps deposits at one month's rent (HSTPA 2019), requires an itemized list of damages and costs, and gives tenants inspection rights.[49] NYC's FARE Act (in force June 11, 2025) stops landlords passing their broker fees to tenants and requires fee disclosure.[79]
- Federal agencies are acting on fees and algorithms. Greystar agreed to pay $24 million over hidden fees (FTC and Colorado, December 2025).[29] The DOJ settled with RealPage (November 2025).[27]
- The UK has a formal process for this. In the year to March 2025, 4,706,470 deposits were protected in England and Wales, and 46,950 went to adjudication, "exactly 1.00%".[78]

Implication: rules about evidence, deadlines and fair deductions reward deterministic compliance checks, which Handoff already plans. They also make each mistake a legal liability.

### 2.6 Operating companies with balance-sheet risk mostly failed; AI roll-ups are the new bet

- Failures and forced exits: Zeus Living raised about $150 million of debt and equity and shut down in 2023 after telling landlords it would "no longer be able to make payments on homes".[32] Frontdesk managed 1,000+ furnished units and laid off all 200 staff in January 2024 after it failed to raise money.[33] Marriott ended its Sonder licence in November 2025 because Sonder defaulted.[34] Divvy sold to Brookfield for about $1 billion against a $2.3 billion peak.[36] Doorstead's founders said openly that guaranteeing rent means covering the shortfall.[37]
- Tech-enabled property managers were consolidated. Mynd merged into Roofstock in 2024.[7] Pathlight's homes moved to Tricon on January 27, 2025.[42] Evernest bought Poplar Homes in 2025 and now operates in 50 markets.[43] Roofstock then invested in Casago's purchase of Vacasa (May 2025).[35]
- A new wave in 2025–2026: Alpine Investors launched Oakline, a private-equity platform that buys property and association management firms.[44] Dwelly (UK) buys letting agencies and runs them on AI. It raised $170 million ($95 million equity plus $75 million debt) in July 2026, manages 15,000 properties, and says one manager now handles 300 units instead of 100.[58][59] Rising Tide (US, seed round, September 2026) builds a "federation of independent property management companies" that keep their brands and receive shared AI and capital.[57] Venn bought Zuma for $50 million in September 2026 and says "We give the software away", charging for "agentic outcomes".[56]
- Market structure supports roll-ups: no US residential property manager has more than 5% share (IBISWorld).[87] In the 2015 RHFS, individual investors owned 74.4% of rental properties and 47.8% of rental units.[77] Newer ownership data was not retrieved (see coverage gaps).

Implication: owning the operator lets a company capture the whole management fee and prove what AI does to margins. But the successful cases are either very young (Dwelly, Rising Tide) or backed by private equity. The failures took on property or rent risk. Buying a management firm is different from leasing homes, but both need capital and operating skill.

### 2.7 Where expansion after a first product worked, and where it did not

Worked (as far as public evidence shows):
- EliseAI went from leasing to maintenance, renewals and delinquency, then into healthcare. It reached $100 million ARR in early 2025.[1] Sacra estimates $200 million ARR in May 2026,[2] and in August 2026 EliseAI was in talks at a $3.7 billion valuation.[1] The expansion added workflows for the same large buyers.
- PMS vendors moved from software into payments, insurance and screening. That is AppFolio's 76% (see 2.1).[16]
- Bilt went from a rent credit card to a loyalty platform with "partnerships with 70% of the nation's top 100 property managers". It is now expanding into condo and HOA buildings, and was valued at $10.75 billion in July 2025.[41]
- Snappt went from fraud detection to "applicant trust" by buying Trigo, backed by a $50 million debt facility.[82]
- Similar operations in neighbouring markets are getting funded. Vantaca (HOA management software) raised $300 million+ at $1.25 billion in October 2025 and serves 500+ management companies and six million households.[53] Breezeway (short-term-rental operations, including housekeeping and vendor coordination; 3,500+ clients) took a PE growth investment in March 2026.[38]

Did not work, or unclear:
- SmartRent's 2025 revenue fell 13% to $152.3 million, although ARR grew.[30] Latch had to restate its financials and was delisted.[31] Hardware-plus-software expansion was hard.
- Plentific raised $100 million in 2021 to take its UK maintenance marketplace to the US.[45] I found no public outcome for the US push (coverage gap). Fixflo, a UK maintenance tool used for about 1.2 million properties, was sold to the European PMS group Aareon.[46]
- Common exit: point tools get absorbed. Knock went to RealPage (2022).[88] Colleen went to Entrata (2024),[24] and LiveEasy to AppFolio (2024).[17] Mezo went to Property Meld (2025).[5] Azibo went to TurboTenant (2025).[72] Zuma went to Venn for $50 million (2026).[56]

### 2.8 Capital climate

- Proptech venture funding was $16.7 billion in 2025, up 67.9%. But $12 billion went into rounds of $100 million or more, and 35 companies took 71.9% of the total.[54] Fifth Wall's CEO called the prior period an "extinction" event.[55]
- Recent seed-stage AI-native PMS entrants include MagicDoor ($4.5 million seed, 2025)[85] and YC's Brickwise ("AI property manager for landlords and estate agents", Fall 2025).[84] Capital for small-landlord finance continues: Baselane raised $34.4 million.[83]

### 2.9 Turn economics (context, mostly claims)

- Rent Ready says an average apartment sits vacant 34.4 days between residents, about $2,200 of lost rent at $66 a day, plus $3,500–$5,000 of make-ready.[51] It is a turn-services vendor. RealPage's analytics found vacant days were nearly five days above the pre-pandemic average (February 2025).[86]
- Invitation Homes (single-family) had a 22.8% annual turnover rate and 0.8% bad debt in 2025. It managed 15,866 homes for others.[52] It launched third-party management with an initial 14,000+ home agreement.[74]

## 3. Expansion options (own taxonomy)

The taxonomy is organized by what grows: depth on one event, breadth of projects, the segment, the party served, the business model, or the company's shape. I scored each against three tests. First, does it build on what these two founders can do (fast AI-agent building, quantitative evaluation, introductions and walkthroughs in Charlottesville and New York)? Second, does it compound the responsibility-plus-money position? Third, is it defensible against the incumbents' bundles?

### A. Segment choice: scattered-site operators with no on-site maintenance
- What: focus the first product on property managers whose turns depend on outside vendors. These are single-family and small multifamily fee managers, SFR institutional and third-party managers, and small NYC buildings.
- Who pays: fee-based property managers (per turn or per door), or SFR operators (per turn or share of spend).
- Why these founders: email-based vendor coordination is Handoff's natural channel. Charlottesville and NYC introductions reach small managers first.
- Evidence: Invitation Homes' 22.8% turnover and its move into third-party management.[52][74] Lessen sells turns to SFR operators.[76] Rentvine raised money with an open API for this segment.[40] Evernest, Oakline and Rising Tide are consolidating it.[43][44][57] Large multifamily already has on-site teams, plus HappyCo and Entrata make-ready tools.[14][23]
- Key risks: small managers pay little and churn [unverified]; Lessen and Lula-style networks are already there;[3][39] per-turn volume per customer may be low.
- Would falsify: pilot data showing most turns at target customers are done by in-house staff, or that coordination time is a small share of turn cost.
- Confidence: medium.

### B. Breadth: from move-out to every episodic, vendor-heavy, owner-funded project
- What: reuse the same loop (gather documents, get quotes, propose a plan and budget, get approval, order, track, pay) for capital replacements, insurance and damage repairs, renovations between tenancies, and code-compliance work.
- Who pays: the same property manager, often passing the cost to owners as a project fee [unverified]. Or a share of project spend.
- Why these founders: it is the same engine and the same buyer, so the marginal product cost is low. Owen's quantitative evaluation carries over.
- Evidence: HappyCo is adding capital planning to maintenance;[14] RealPage and Lessen sell spend management and capital projects.[28][76] Incumbents' AI mostly targets reactive tickets, not multi-vendor projects.[15][23]
- Key risks: projects are rarer than turns; restoration and insurance work has specialist incumbents [unverified]; scope creep.
- Would falsify: operators reporting that non-turn projects are handled by owners directly or by one general contractor, leaving little coordination work.
- Confidence: medium.

### C. Business model: charge on outcomes and on money flow
- What: price per completed turn, per day of vacancy saved, or as a small share of the vendor spend Handoff orders and pays. Later, earn on payment rails (vendor payouts, deposit refunds).
- Who pays: property managers or owners out of turn budgets. Payment revenue comes from transaction fees.
- Why these founders: Handoff already runs vendor payments inside owner rules, and its deterministic money checks make this credible.
- Evidence: most incumbent revenue is transactional (AppFolio about 76%);[16] Venn charges for outcomes and gives the software away;[56] Lessen's CEO pointed to non-take-rate revenue as the "big unlock", which shows the limits of take-rate alone.[3]
- Key risks: payments need money-transmitter or partner-bank arrangements [unverified]; PMSs defend payments; many property managers earn maintenance markups and may resist cost transparency [unverified].
- Would falsify: pilot customers refusing outcome pricing, or PMS payment terms blocking outside payouts.
- Confidence: medium.

### D. Depth: settlement tied to the evidence the turn already produced
- What: the planned tenant-account settlement, positioned as "every charge backed by the turn's own quotes, invoices and photos, with state-rule checks". Then recovery of balances owed.
- Who pays: property managers (per move-out), or a share of recovered funds.
- Why these founders: it joins two workflows that competitors sell separately.
- Evidence: the settlement space is crowded. Obligo's Deposit Agent, Roost and Pay Ready are live.[11][61][60] Entrata owns Colleen's recovery product.[24][47] Documentation drives recovery.[70] Regulation demands itemized evidence.[48][50]
- Key risks: debt-collection rules (FDCPA/Reg F) if Handoff collects on others' behalf [unverified]; direct conflict with embedded deposit providers; legal liability for wrong deductions.
- Would falsify: operators saying they would keep their existing deposit or recovery vendor, or that dispute rates are already low.
- Confidence: medium that it is needed to finish the core; low that it is a separate growth engine.

### E. Different shape: own or federate the operator (AI-native management firm)
- What: buy, or partner with, one or more small property management firms in Charlottesville or NYC and run them on Handoff. Earn the management fee, not a software fee.
- Who pays: owners, through management fees.
- Why these founders: it proves the unit economics directly and removes the question of the PMS buyer's willingness to pay. Connor's introductions fit acquisition sourcing.
- Evidence: Dwelly's $170 million round and its 300-units-per-manager claim.[58] Rising Tide's federation.[57] Oakline and Evernest.[44][43] A fragmented market.[87] Counter-evidence: Mynd, Pathlight and Frontdesk.[7][42][33]
- Key risks: capital; real-estate licensing for managing for others in VA/NY [unverified]; trust-accounting liability; the founders' operating experience is unknown; service quality with a thin team (Pathlight's handover).
- Would falsify: acquisition multiples too high for the capital available, or pilot data showing staff leverage well under 2x.
- Confidence: low now; medium if pilots show large staff leverage and capital is available.

### F. Segment: seasonal mass-turn operators (student housing)
- What: target operators whose turns cluster into a few weeks a year, where coordination is the bottleneck.
- Who pays: student-housing operators and managers of college-town rentals.
- Why these founders: Charlottesville is a university town [unverified that its rental stock skews to students]. A seasonal peak gives a fast, measurable test.
- Evidence: Entrata lists student housing as a core vertical;[25] its make-ready boards already target turns.[23] I found no direct evidence on the economics of student-housing turn seasons (coverage gap).
- Key risks: the enterprise student operators run on Entrata; revenue is seasonal; smaller college-town managers may do turns in-house.
- Would falsify: local operators saying the August turn is handled by fixed vendor contracts with little coordination.
- Confidence: low-medium.

### G. Party: claims adjudication for deposit-alternative and guarantee insurers
- What: sell move-out charge verification (evidence plus rule check) to firms that pay claims when tenants leave.
- Who pays: LeaseLock, Obligo, TheGuarantors-type firms, and renters-insurance carriers.
- Why these founders: Handoff's reasoning over lease, condition and invoice evidence is exactly what claims teams need.
- Evidence: the size of insured deposit exposure (LeaseLock, $14 billion of leases);[9] Obligo building its own agent shows the value, and also that the largest player will build rather than buy.[11]
- Key risks: few buyers; long insurer sales cycles; build-versus-buy.
- Would falsify: two or three insurers saying claims cost is not a priority, or that claims are already automated.
- Confidence: low.

### H. Adjacent vertical: community associations (HOA/condo)
- What: the same "owner's funds and instructions plus vendor projects" loop for association managers.
- Who pays: association management companies.
- Evidence: Vantaca's $1.25 billion valuation;[53] Bilt expanding into condo and HOA;[41] Oakline combining property and association management.[44]
- Key risks: Vantaca dominates; a different buyer; the founders have no stated access.
- Confidence: low.

### I. Geography: UK or other markets with formal deposit schemes
- What: take settlement and turns to markets where deposit adjudication is standardized.
- Evidence: UK volumes and a 1% adjudication rate;[78] Dwelly's AI roll-up in the UK;[58] a UK-to-US expansion (Plentific) with no visible outcome.[45]
- Key risks: no founder access; a different legal system; well-funded local players.
- Confidence: low.

### Options I rank as poor fits
- AI leasing, showing or renewals: EliseAI, Zuma/Venn and every PMS are there.[1][56][15]
- Routine maintenance intake: Property Meld and Latchel are established.[5][4] Vendoroo and the PMS agents cover the rest.[75][15]
- Building a vendor marketplace: operations-heavy; Lessen and Lula lead.[3][39] Vendors already use trade software such as ServiceTitan, which IPO'd in December 2024.[73]
- Deposit replacement or rent guarantees: insurer capital; balance-sheet risk.[9][37]
- Tenant-side consumer products: Bilt and Flex own this with large capital.[41][81]

## 4. Ranking and reasoning

1. A + B together. Choose scattered-site operators, then widen to all episodic, vendor-funded projects for them. This compounds the one scarce asset: an agent that accepts responsibility for vendor work paid from the owner's money. It avoids the bundled ticket and chat layer and does not need Yardi access.
2. C. Outcome and money-flow pricing. This is not a separate direction; it is the way to monetize A and B. Incumbents' income shows the money is in flows.[16]
3. D. Finish settlement, but as evidence-linked settlement, not a standalone deposit product. It is needed for completeness. As a separate market it is crowded.[11][61][60]
4. E. AI-native operator or federation. It has the highest upside and the highest risk. Treat it as a gated option: only consider it after pilots measure staff leverage and if the founders can raise acquisition capital. The 2026 funding shows investors will pay for this shape.[58][57] The 2023–2025 failures show how it breaks.[33][42]
5. F. The student-housing season: a cheap test that uses the Charlottesville connection.
6. G, H, I. Keep as later options. Revisit only with specific evidence.

Why this order: first, where do incumbents have little reason or ability to follow? Accountability for spending the owner's money is a liability they avoid [unverified]. Second, where does the founders' access help? Small managers in their two cities. Third, what can a two-person team run? Software-led service, not a vendor network or balance sheet.

## 5. Challenges to the premise

- The expansion question may be premature. There are no pilot customers yet. The evidence says the choice of segment and unit of sale decides outcomes more than the sequence of expansions. Frontdesk, Zeus and Mynd had expansion plans; they failed or were merged on unit economics.[33][32][7]
- The core may be squeezed before any expansion. Entrata's make-ready boards,[23] AppFolio's maintenance performer[15] and Obligo's deposit agent[11] all touch the planned scope. So do the 2026 turn tools such as TurnOps and Market Ready Turn.[63][64] TurnBoard, APTS and Frontdesk overlap too.[65][66][67] The real question may be "what makes turnover-with-responsibility defensible?", not "where next?".
- A realistic outcome may be a tuck-in sale. Most comparable AI point tools ended as acquisitions by a PMS or platform, and the one priced publicly sold for $50 million.[56] If that is acceptable, expansion should target what an acquirer lacks, such as settlement linked to turns and vendor payments, not breadth.
- The unit of value may be the owner's outcome (days vacant, turn cost, deposit disputes), not the property manager's labour. Property managers may earn money from the inefficiency, through maintenance fees and markups [unverified]. If so, the best buyer could be owners or SFR asset managers, and Handoff's sales story changes.
- The demo platform choice may matter for SMB pricing. If production stays on an enterprise data platform, per-turn cost may not fit small-manager budgets [unverified]. This is outside my lane but affects option A.

## 6. Founder facts my conclusions depend on

- Capital raised and runway. Option E needs substantial equity or debt; A to C do not.
- Full-time status. A service-like product with accountability for money needs fast responses during turns.
- Licences and operating history. Managing property for others generally needs a real-estate licence in Virginia and New York [unverified]. Without one, E means acquiring licensed firms.
- Depth of Connor's network in Charlottesville and NYC. Options A and F rely on warm introductions to small managers and student operators.
- Tolerance for regulated money activities. Option C (payments) and D (recovery) may need licences or partners.
- Willingness to be acquired versus building an independent company. This changes whether breadth or depth wins.

## 7. Questions only private operator data can answer

1. Per door per year: how many turns, and what share of turn work goes to outside vendors versus in-house staff?
2. Staff hours per turn, split by task: scoping, quotes, scheduling, follow-up, invoices, owner approvals, deposit statements.
3. Days vacant per turn that come from coordination delay, as opposed to scope or materials.
4. Distribution of turn cost; how often owners reject or change the proposed budget.
5. Maintenance markups or coordination fees the manager earns, and how much of the manager's margin depends on them.
6. Deposit outcomes: share of deposits with deductions, dispute rate, small-claims cases, and recovery rate on balances owed.
7. Which PMS and plan tier each target manager uses (API access), and whether they would allow outside vendor payouts.
8. Frequency and size of non-turn projects (capital replacements, insurance repairs, compliance work).
9. Willingness to pay per turn, per door, or as a share of spend, and who signs (manager or owner).
10. For possible acquisition targets: doors, management fee rate, staff count, owner churn, and asking multiple.
