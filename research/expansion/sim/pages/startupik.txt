Funding & Investment What Are Your Real Odds of Raising a Series A in 2026? Seed-to-Series-A graduation rates, timing and benchmarks for 2026: what the cohort data really shows, why the headline numbers are stale, and the ARR, burn multiple and headcount bar you actually need. But that headline number is the wrong one to plan against. It is measured on cohorts that raised seed in 2022 and 2023, the worst funding window on record, and it is mathematically guaranteed to rise as those companies keep converting. The number that describes conditions right now — the share of a seed cohort reaching Series A within twelve months — has roughly doubled since 2023, from 4 to 5 percent to 10 or 11 percent, according to data from Carta. Last reviewed: September 2026 Quick Answer: what the 2026 data actually says Carta reported that about 17 percent of the 2022 seed cohort had raised a Series A within 24 months, against 25 to 30 percent for the 2018 cohort. Carta’s twelve-month leading indicator has improved two years running: 9 percent of the Q2 2024 seed cohort reached Series A inside one year, described by Carta as the highest rate since Q3 2021, and Peter Walker of Carta put the 2025 cohort at 10 to 11 percent using Q1 2026 data. Carta published two different medians for the seed-to-Series-A interval: 774 days for Q4 2024 and 616 days for Q2 2025. Both are computed only on companies that raised an A. The Series A revenue bar is now $2 million to $5 million in ARR , with a 2025 median around $2.5 million, per venture firm CRV — roughly 75 percent above 2021 levels. Median headcount at Series A fell from 25.9 employees in 2021 to about 16 in 2025 , per Carta’s State of Seed data as reported by SaaStr, while the revenue bar rose. Down rounds fell to 11.4 percent of Carta-recorded fundings in Q1 2026, down from a 22 percent peak in 2023. Fewer companies clear the bar, but the terms behind it are the best in three years. What does “graduation rate” actually measure? A graduation rate is the share of companies from one funding cohort that go on to raise the next round. The cohort is defined by when the seed round closed. The measurement window is how long the analyst waited before counting. Almost every article on this topic reports the first half and drops the second. That omission is what makes the numbers useless for planning. Consider two figures from Carta. About 30.6 percent of companies that raised seed in Q1 2018 reached a Series A within two years. About 17 percent of the 2022 cohort did. Both are real. They are not comparable to a 2024 cohort figure measured at eighteen months, because the 2024 cohort has not finished converting. Crunchbase illustrated the trap in its own archive. In January 2025 it stated that the 2023 and 2024 cohorts were “too recent to analyze.” In May 2026 it published graduation rates for exactly those cohorts: 24 percent for 2023 and 16 percent for 2024. The 16 percent figure is measured against a market whose own median conversion time exceeds two years. It is arithmetically certain to rise. What is right-censoring, and why does it matter here? Right-censoring is what happens when you measure an outcome before the observation period has finished. Half the companies in a young cohort have not had time to succeed or fail yet, so counting them as failures understates the true rate. Every graduation rate for a cohort younger than about four years is right-censored. Nobody in the competitor set says so. A founder reading “16 percent” concludes the odds are one in six. The honest statement is “16 percent so far, with most of the conversion window still open.” How long does a seed round actually have to last? Here the published data contradicts itself, and the contradiction has gone unnoticed for a year. In March 2025 Carta reported the median time between seed and Series A as 774 days, about 2.1 years, in Q4 2024 — an 84 percent increase on the 420-day median of Q4 2021. In September 2025 Carta reported the median seed-to-Series-A interval as 616 days, approximately 20 months, in Q2 2025. That is a 158-day gap between two figures from the same data provider, six months apart. The 774-day number is the one that propagated. It still appears in benchmark guides published in September 2026, labelled as a current figure, with no mention that Carta itself later published something shorter. Both figures share a deeper problem. They are computed exclusively over companies that raised a Series A. The median wait for a randomly chosen seed company is not 616 days or 774 days. It is undefined, because most seed companies never raise an A at all. Every “plan for 2.1 years of runway” recommendation in circulation is built on a statistic derived only from winners. What does the distribution look like, not just the median? SaaStr, analysing a Carta dataset of 3,365 US startups that raised a Series A between Q1 2018 and Q3 2025, found that the share reaching an A in under roughly 18 to 24 months fell from 41 percent in Q3 2019 to 23 percent in Q3 2025. Over the same period, the share taking three years or more rose from 19 percent to 39 percent. Only about 15 percent get there within twelve months. Carta’s State of Seed data, as reported by SaaStr, puts the 75th percentile at 1,251 days — about 3.4 years. That tail is the planning number. If you raise a seed round sized for two years and you land at the 75th percentile, you run out of money fourteen months before the round arrives. Is the market getting better or worse in 2026? Both, depending on which end of the distribution you are at. The headlines say crunch. The leading indicators say thaw. In September 2025 Carta reported that 9 percent of the Q2 2024 seed cohort had reached Series A within a single year, calling it the highest such rate since Q3 2021. Peter Walker, Carta’s Head of Insights, gave Q1 2026 figures putting the one-year rate at 10 to 11 percent for the 2025 cohort against 4 to 5 percent in 2022 and 2023. Carta’s own commentary in March 2026 stated that it was much harder to graduate in 2023 than in 2021, and than it is likely to be this year. Meanwhile, an August 2026 article headlined “The Series A Crunch Is Back: Why 85% of Seed-Stage Startups Now Fail” was built on a figure of 15.4 percent drawn from Carta’s Q3 2024 report, measuring the early-2022 cohort at 24 months. The word “now” in that headline describes companies that raised seed four and a half years earlier. The twenty-four-month graduation rates everyone quotes are lagging indicators describing 2022 and 2023 fundraising conditions. The twelve-month rates describe today, and they are moving in the opposite direction. Why are fewer doors opening onto better terms? Carta recorded a Series A median pre-money valuation of $47.9 million in Q2 2025, a new high and up 20 percent year on year, in the same quarter that Series A deal count fell 18 percent and dollars raised fell 23 percent. Down rounds dropped to 11.4 percent of fundings in Q1 2026 from a 22 percent peak in 2023. Median dilution from seed to Series C fell from about 18 percent to 16 percent year on year, and Series B dilution from about 15 percent to 12.9 percent. Marcos Fernandez of Fiat Ventures, quoted by Carta, summarised it: quantity is down and quality is up. The practical reading is that the Series A market is not contracting uniformly. It is bifurcating. Fewer companies clear the bar, and the ones that do are getting better prices and giving up less of the company than at any point since 2022. What does a startup actually need to raise a Series A in 2026? Annual recurring revenue, or ARR, is the annualised value of a company’s contracted subscription revenue. It is the metric most Series A investors index on for software businesses. CRV, an independent venture firm, published the current range as $2 million to $5 million in ARR, equivalent to roughly $167,000 to $417,000 in monthly recurring revenue, with the median reaching about $2.5 million in 2025 — around 75 percent higher than 2021. Crunchbase put the threshold at $2 million to $3 million, sometimes $4 million, against a previous $1 million standard. Burn multiple is net cash burned divided by net new ARR added over the same period. A burn multiple of one means the company spent one dollar to add one dollar of new annual recurring revenue. The framework in general use, popularised by investor David Sacks, treats below 1.0x as exceptional, 1.0x to 1.5x as great, 1.5x to 2.0x as good, 2.0x to 3.0x as suspect and above 3.0x as bad. CRV describes burn multiple as the defining efficiency metric at Series A and expects strong, sustained double-digit year-on-year growth from companies in the $1 million to $5 million ARR band. Be careful with the burn-multiple benchmark tables circulating online. The most widely shared one carries no publication date, no sample size and no stated methodology. Treat the Sacks bands as a rule of thumb and CRV as the sourced authority. Is 120 percent net revenue retention really the floor? No, and this is the single most damaging number in circulation. Net revenue retention, or NRR, is the share of last year’s revenue retained from the same set of customers, including expansion and net of churn and downgrades. A survey of 52 venture funds published in April 2026 reported an NRR “floor” of 120 percent or more at Series A. CRV’s observed data says something very different: top-quartile companies at $1 million to $3 million of ARR achieve approximately 94 percent NRR, and companies at $3 million to $15 million reach approximately 99 percent. The number being circulated as a minimum sits above what the best companies in the relevant revenue band actually achieve. One figure is what investors say they want in a survey. The other is measured performance. A founder at 95 percent NRR and $2.5 million ARR is being told they are below the floor when they are in fact top quartile. SaaS Capital’s fifteenth annual survey of more than 1,000 private B2B SaaS companies found median growth of 22 percent in 2025, down from 25 percent in 2024, with bootstrapped companies at 20 percent and equity-backed at 25 percent. It also found that growth correlates exponentially with NRR: moving from the 90 to 100 percent band into the 100 to 110 percent band improves growth by roughly five percentage points. Why has revenue per employee at Series A roughly quadrupled? This is the finding that reframes everything above, and no one in the ranking set has computed it. The ARR bar moved from about $1 million in 2021 to a $2.5 million median in 2025. Median headcount at Series A fell from 25.9 in 2021 to roughly 16 over the same period. Divide one by the other. In 2021 a median Series A company was running about $39,000 of ARR per employee. In 2025 it is running about $150,000. That is close to a fourfold shift in four years, and it happened on both sides of the ratio at once. Seed headcount fell in parallel, from 10.3 to 6.2. The constraint is not “generate more revenue.” It is “generate substantially more revenue without the headcount that used to be how you generated revenue.” That is exactly why burn multiple, not raw ARR, became the gating metric. An investor looking at a fifteen-person company with $2.5 million of ARR is not admiring the revenue. They are admiring that it took fifteen people. What is suppressing the graduation rate that has nothing to do with your company? The denominator is growing faster than the numerator. The PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor for Q2 2026 estimated 5,674 first-venture-round deals in the first half of 2026, putting the year on pace for more than 10,000 companies raising a first round — which would be a record. Carta simultaneously recorded 4,859 rounds across its platform in 2025, the lowest count in six years. More companies entering the funnel while Series A capacity stays flat or falls means the graduation percentage drops as an arithmetic consequence. No individual company got worse. Data cited by Chronograph from SVB’s State of the Markets shows seed deals outpacing Series A deals by roughly three to one, which is the same phenomenon viewed from the other end. Do the medians describe a market you are actually in? Probably not. Crunchbase reported that over 40 percent of all seed and Series A investment in 2026 has gone to rounds of $100 million or more, and that for US startups specifically, more than half of all seed and Series A funding this year went to financings of $100 million and up. Named examples include a $480 million seed for Humans&, a $252 million seed for Merge Labs, a $300 million Series A for Ricursive Intelligence and a $200 million Series A for Upscale AI. PitchBook-NVCA put megadeals of $100 million or more at 87.5 percent of all H1 2026 venture dollars, with AI accounting for 86 percent of dollars invested. When a seed round can be $480 million, “median seed round size” stops functioning as a planning number for an ordinary company. Carta’s data also shows 66 percent of top-decile seed valuations going to startups in San Francisco (44 percent) or New York (22 percent). Expert Insight: Ali Hajimohamadi Ali Hajimohamadi is an entrepreneur, startup educator and the founder of Startupik. Founders treat the graduation rate as a probability that applies to them. It does not. It is a population statistic dominated by companies that raised seed capital without a plan for what the A requires, and you either are one of those or you are not. Knowing which is a question you can answer in an afternoon by dividing your ARR by your headcount. The runway advice in circulation is backwards. Everyone says raise 24 to 36 months because the interval got longer. The interval got longer partly because companies had more money and moved slower. Capital buys time, and time spent without revenue growth is the thing that kills the round, not the absence of capital. I would rather see a team of six at $1.5 million of ARR than a team of twenty at $3 million. The second one clears the published bar and will still struggle, because the burn multiple tells the investor exactly how much more money it takes to buy the next dollar. The bifurcation is the real story of 2026 and most founders are reading it as bad news. Fewer Series A rounds at higher valuations with lower dilution is a market that pays more for the companies that qualify. If you are going to be told no, you want to be told no in a market like this one rather than in one where everybody gets a mediocre yes. Watch the twelve-month cohort numbers, not the twenty-four-month ones. Anyone quoting you a 2022-cohort statistic in late 2026 is describing a market that no longer exists, and they are usually doing it to sell you something. The hardest discipline is refusing to hire against a round you have not raised. Every extra head is a permanent increase in the revenue you must show, because the bar is now expressed per employee whether or not anyone says so out loud. Who should not optimise for a Series A at all? The benchmarks above describe one specific path. Several kinds of company are better off ignoring them. Businesses growing at 20 to 30 percent a year with positive cash flow are penalised by Series A expectations that assume triple-digit growth. SaaS Capital’s data shows median private B2B SaaS growth at 22 percent — a perfectly healthy business that would fail a Series A screen. Companies outside San Francisco and New York without a category-defining story face a valuation environment where the top decile is geographically concentrated. Raising a smaller round from investors who price your market correctly beats chasing a coastal median. Founders with 56.2 percent ownership after seed, falling to 36.1 percent after a Series A according to Carta’s dilution curve, should price what the round actually costs. A company that can reach profitability at $4 million of ARR without another round keeps roughly 20 percentage points more of itself. Frequently asked questions What percentage of startups raise a Series A? Roughly 16 to 24 percent of recent US seed cohorts have progressed beyond seed so far, per Crunchbase, against 51 to 61 percent for cohorts through 2020. Carta put the 2022 cohort at about 17 percent within 24 months. All recent-cohort figures will rise, because most of those companies are still inside their conversion window. How long does it take to raise a Series A after seed? Carta has published both 774 days (Q4 2024) and 616 days (Q2 2025) as the median interval. The 75th percentile is about 1,251 days, or 3.4 years. Both medians are computed only over companies that succeeded, so treat them as the best case rather than the expected case. How much ARR do I need for a Series A in 2026? CRV puts the range at $2 million to $5 million with a 2025 median near $2.5 million. Crunchbase reports $2 million to $4 million. Neither is a hard gate — capital efficiency and growth rate can compensate below the range, and neither compensates for a burn multiple above 3.0x. Is the Series A market improving in 2026? On the leading indicators, yes. Carta’s one-year graduation rate roughly doubled from 4 to 5 percent in 2022 and 2023 to 10 to 11 percent for the 2025 cohort. Down rounds fell to 11.4 percent in Q1 2026 from a 22 percent peak in 2023, and median dilution fell year on year. Why do graduation rates keep getting revised upward? Because cohorts keep converting after the measurement is taken. A rate published at eighteen months of exposure, in a market whose median conversion time exceeds two years, is a partial count presented as a final one. Does an AI company have better odds? On price, clearly. Carta found Series A AI startups carried median valuations 38 percent higher than non-AI peers, and PitchBook-NVCA attributed 86 percent of H1 2026 venture dollars to AI. Carta’s Q1 2026 data showed more than 60 cents of every venture dollar on its platform going to AI companies. Whether the graduation rate itself is higher is not something the published cohort data separates out. How much runway should a seed round buy? Plan against the 75th percentile of 3.4 years, not the median. The specific dollar target matters less than keeping the burn multiple under control while you get there, because a longer runway bought with a larger team raises the revenue bar you have to clear at the end of it. Final Recommendation Stop planning against the graduation rate and start planning against the two numbers that determine whether you are in it: revenue per employee and burn multiple. The market now prices a Series A at roughly $150,000 of ARR per head and a burn multiple under 1.5x, and those two figures explain the ARR threshold, the falling headcount and the rising valuations all at once. Size your seed round against 3.4 years, not 2.1, and resist spending the difference on headcount. Every hire raises the revenue you must show at the end. Ignore any 2026 article quoting a 24-month graduation rate as a current condition. That figure describes the 2022 and 2023 market. The twelve-month indicator has been improving for two years, down rounds are at a three-year low and dilution is falling. Fewer companies will clear the bar this year, and the ones that do will get the best terms since 2021.
