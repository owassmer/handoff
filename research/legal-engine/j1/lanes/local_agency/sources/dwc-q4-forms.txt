L49@P1: INTRODUCTION
L50@P1: This clinical practice guideline presents evidence-based recommendations for assessing and
L51@P1: treating adults with traumatic brain injury (TBI). Topics include the initial assessment and
L52@P1: diagnosis of patients with TBI, identification of red flags that may suggest the presence of a
L53@P1: serious underlying medical condition, initial clinical evaluation, management, diagnostic
L54@P1: considerations and special studies to identify clinical pathology, work-relatedness, modified
L55@P1: duty and activity, rehabilitative strategies, and return to work, as well as further
L56@P1: management considerations including delayed recovery.
L57@P1: Guidance is provided on the treatment of working-age adults who have sustained TBI, as
L58@P1: well as the evaluation and management of symptoms ranging from acute/subacute to
L59@P1-2: chronic. The primary target users of this guideline are clinicians. Although the primary Copyright ©2025 Reed Group, LLC 3
L60@P2: patient population is working adults, the principles may apply more comprehensively. This
L61@P2: guideline does not address several broad categories, including the impact of
L62@P2: cerebrovascular accidents, concomitant congenital disorders, or malignancies. It also does
L63@P2: not address specific intraoperative procedures.
L64@P2: The objectives of this TBI guideline include baseline evaluations, diagnostic tests and
L65@P2: imaging, physical activity, return to work, medications, physical and occupational therapy,
L66@P2: injections, and rehabilitation. Comparative effectiveness is addressed where available. This
L67@P2: guideline does not address comprehensive psychological and behavioral aspects of pain
L68@P2: management; these are addressed separately in the ACOEM Chronic Pain guideline. Cervical
L69@P2: spine pain and related disorders are addressed in the ACOEM Cervical and Thoracic Spine
L70@P2: Disorders guideline.

L75@P2: ● What diagnostic approaches and special studies identify clinical pathology?
L76@P2: ● What initial treatment approaches have evidence of efficacy?
L77@P2: ● What is the evidence of work-relatedness for various diagnoses?
L78@P2: ● What modified duty and activity prescriptions and limitations are effective and
L79@P2: recommended?
L80@P2: ● When is return-to-work status recommended?
L81@P2: ● When initial treatment options fail, what evidence supports other interventions?
L82@P2: ● When and for what conditions are injections and other invasive procedures
L83@P2: recommended?
L84@P2: ● What management options are recommended for delayed recovery?

L110@P3: IMPACT
L111@P3: The global incidence of traumatic brain injury (TBI) has been estimated at 69 million people
L112@P3: annually (11). There were 214,110 TBI-related hospitalizations in the US in 2020 and 69,473
L113@P3: TBI-related deaths in 2021 (12, 13). The crude incidence rate of TBI is much more difficult to
L114@P3: measure because the overwhelming majority of cases are mild TBI/concussion cases
L115@P3: (estimated at 95% (14)) and are treated as outpatients, if they are evaluated and treated at
L116@P3: all; therefore, there is a wide range of estimates in epidemiological studies. A meta-analysis
L117@P3: estimated the global annual incidence rate at 295 per 100,000 (15). The adjusted risk among
L118@P3: males is uniformly higher, and the rate among US males is 3.3-fold greater (16).
L119@P3: It is believed that factors such as automobile safety, seat belt use, helmet use, and better
L120@P3: overall treatment for severe TBI in prehospital and hospital settings (with a focus on
L121@P3: avoiding hypoxia, hypotension, and hyperventilation), while unable to prevent TBI entirely,
L122@P3: have somewhat mitigated the impacts and severity of TBI and thus mortality. Yet,
L123@P3: prospective cohort data also indicate an additional challenge in that the mortality risk is 81%
L124@P3: elevated over a 14-year period after sustaining a TBI, although it did not distinguish
L125@P3: between mild, moderate, and severe TBI (17).
L126@P3: A systematic review estimated that 17.9% of TBI cases were work-related, 76% occurred in
L127@P3: males, the mean age was 40.4 years, and 3.6% resulted in death (18). A population-based
L128@P3: study of worker's compensation claims in Ohio found a rate of 51/100,000 FTEs; 40% had
L129@P3: lost work time and the highest rates were among spectator sports and general freight
L130@P3: trucking (19). A Korean study of workers compensation insurance data reported a workrelated TBI incidence rate of 28.4/100,000, of which the mild rate was 12.9/100,000 and
L131@P3: 15.5/100,000 were moderate to severe; construction was the most common industry
L132@P3: (39.7%), followed by 19.7% from manufacturing; 86.4% had lost work time; 91.2% sustained
L133@P3: an inpatient hospital stay; the median duration of work disability was 148 days; and the
L134@P3: mean total claim cost was $45,082 (20). A Montreal trauma center-based study indicated
L135@P3: most work-related TBI cases occurred in construction industry, the most common
L136@P3: mechanism of injury was a fall, and 76.8% were mild TBI cases (21). Another systematic
L137@P3: review found falls to be the most common mechanism of injury (22).
L138@P3: A U.S. naval health study reported a cumulative prevalence rate for service-related TBI of
L139@P3: 27%, with the highest risk among those serving in "Infantry, Gun Crews, Seamanship"
L140@P3: (OR=1.79, 95% CI 1.58-2.02) (23, 24). Military causes of TBI have been associated with longterm risk of job loss after separation from service (25)
L141@P3: . For TBI among military personnel,
L142@P3: disability estimates vary strongly by severity: 12.0% among those with mild TBI, 31.4% for
L143@P3: those with moderate TBI, 56.2% for penetrating TBI, and 65.6% for severe TBI (26). The
L144@P3: civilian data likely underestimate the prevalence of TBI because they do not include persons
L145@P3: with TBI sequelae who were treated and released from emergency departments, those who
L146@P3-4: sought care in other healthcare settings, and those who did not seek treatment (27, 28). Copyright ©2025 Reed Group, LLC 5
L147@P4: A California database study found that 11.2% of mild TBI claims were due to workplace
L148@P4: violence (29). Although TBI may occur less frequently in the workplace compared to other
L149@P4: injuries, the unique needs of workers who sustained TBI has been noted to include the need
L150@P4: to focus on functional restoration and return to work.
L151@P4: TBI also carries enormous per-capita costs, in large part due to vocational issues of
L152@P4: impairments, employability, and productivity. Annual healthcare costs for all U.S. TBIs have
L153@P4: been estimated at $40.6 billion (30); however, those cost estimates do not include what are
L154@P4: likely substantially greater total costs, including disability, impairments, work lost time, and
L155@P4: reductions in productivity. An estimated 5 million Americans are living with disability from
L156@P4: TBI (31). Between 3.2 and 5.3 million persons (1.1%-1.7% of the U.S. population) live with
L157@P4: long-term disabilities that result from TBI (32).
L158@P4: DEFINITIONS AND RELATED TERMS
L159@P4: Active Therapy: The term "active therapy" is generally thought of as the patient taking an
L160@P4: active role in their treatment via various modalities. Although there is not one specific
L161@P4: treatment defined by this term, it may include psychological, social, and educational
L162@P4: components in conjunction with therapeutic exercises (33). Therapeutic exercises could
