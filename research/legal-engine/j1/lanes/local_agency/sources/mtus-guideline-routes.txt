L83@P2: OVERVIEW
L84@P2: This clinical practice guideline presents recommendations on assessing and treating adults
L85@P2: with shoulder disorders. It is critical to note that “shoulder pain” may often be a symptom of
L86@P2: another disorder in another body part, especially of the cervical spine and thorax. Thus,
L87@P2: careful evaluation to determine the diagnosis and origin of the pain is critical in order to be
L88@P2: able to form a well-founded, evidence-based approach to treatment (see also, e.g., the
L89@P2: ACOEM Cervical and Thoracic Spine Disorders Guideline).
L90@P2: This guideline addresses the following shoulder disorders that commonly present to
L91@P2: physicians: acromioclavicular arthrosis and glenohumeral arthrosis; acromioclavicular
L92@P2: sprain, separation or dislocation; adhesive capsulitis; bicipital tendinitis and tears; brachial
L93@P2: plexus injuries; calcific tendinitis; degenerative joint disease (including osteoarthrosis);
L94@P2: dislocation (glenohumeral); fractures; instability; labral tear; non-specific shoulder pain;
L95@P2: osteonecrosis; rotator cuff syndromes; rotator cuff tears; thoracic outlet syndrome; and
L96@P2: trigger points/myofascial pain.
L97@P2: Topics include: the initial assessment and diagnosis of patients with acute, subacute, and
L98@P2: chronic shoulder disorders with particular emphasis on work-related factors; identification
L99@P2: of red flags that may indicate the presence of a serious underlying medical condition;
L100@P2: diagnostic considerations and special studies for identifying clinical pathology; workrelatedness, return-to-work planning (including work restrictions, modified duty, and
L101@P2: activity level); clinical management; occupational and physical therapy indications; surgical
L102@P2: indications; rehabilitation; and the management of delayed recovery.
L103@P2: Algorithms for patient management are included. The guideline’s master algorithm
L104@P2-3: schematizes a recommended approach by which practitioners may generally manage Copyright ©2025 Reed Group, LLC 4
L105@P3: patients with shoulder disorders. The following text, tables, and numbered algorithms
L106@P3: expand upon the master algorithm.
L107@P3: Acromioclavicular (AC) Arthrosis, Glenohumeral Arthrosis
L108@P3: Arthroses in the acromioclavicular and glenohumeral joints are common, although less
L109@P3: common than those of the hands, knees, and hips. Radiographs show degenerative joint
L110@P3: disease and may suggest an underlying etiology. Etiologies for arthroses include
L111@P3: osteoarthrosis (also known as osteoarthritis), developmental anomalies, rheumatoid
L112@P3: arthritis, other inflammatory rheumatological disorders, crystal diseases, post-infectious
L113@P3: complications, and systemic factors. Most cases are assumed to be degenerative
L114@P3: osteoarthroses with a genetic component.
L115@P3: Acromioclavicular (AC) Sprain, Separation, Dislocation
L116@P3: Sprains involve high-force falls and any type of trauma that produce a disruption of the
L117@P3: ligaments about a joint. Commonly, these injuries occur by direct blow, typically from falling
L118@P3: laterally onto the shoulder, or a fall on an outstretched hand, or direct trauma to the joint.
L119@P3: AC joint separation (“shoulder separation”) and dislocation are more severe than a Grade I
L120@P3: AC joint sprain.
L121@P3: Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder)
L122@P3: Adhesive capsulitisainvolves a reduction in passive range of motion (ROM) of the shoulder
L123@P3: in three or more directions. To fully assess, the affected shoulder’s ROM should be
L124@P3: compared with the unaffected side. Frozen shoulder can be classified as idiopathic adhesive
L125@P3: capsulitis, or secondary to trauma, or underlying shoulder pathology. The most common
L126@P3: cause is idiopathic and associations with diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and female
L127@P3: gender have been reported. Glenohumeral contracture can also occur after traumatic injury,
L128@P3: in association with rotator cuff disorders, or after shoulder surgery.
L129@P3: Bicipital Tendinitis and Tears
L130@P3: Anterior shoulder pain may be caused by bicipital tendinitis. Bicipital tears are believed to
L131@P3: result from pathophysiological mechanisms similar to rotator cuff tears. Many are thought
L132@P3: to be a result of chronic tendinopathy followed by tears while others are a result of an acute
L133@P3: traumatic event typically occurring in the context of a prior degenerative tear, which often
L134@P3: has been asymptomatic. They generally occur in conjunction with rotator cuff pathology.
L135@P3: Another sometimes related but infrequent entity is biceps subluxation and dislocation.
L136@P3: Brachial Plexus Injuries
L137@P3: Brachial plexopathies are caused by forceful stretching or compression of the nerves that
L138@P3: travel from the spine to the upper extremity and are thought to occur after relatively severe
L139@P3: or high-force accidents, falls from heights, and sports (e.g., “stingers”). However, reliable
L140@P3-4: etiological and epidemiological data are not available. Idiopathic brachial plexopathy occurs Copyright ©2025 Reed Group, LLC 5
L141@P4: infrequently, and Parsonage Turner Syndrome should be considered in the differential
L142@P4: diagnosis.
L143@P4: Calcific Tendinitis
L144@P4: There is no consensus as to why calcific tendinitis occurs but some hypotheses include
L145@P4: underlying transformation of the tenocytes into chondrocytes inducing subsequent
L146@P4: calcification within the tendon; abnormal activity of the thyroid gland; metabolic diseases
L147@P4: (e.g., diabetes), and genetic predisposition (1). These calcium deposits are generally found
L148@P4: inside or around the rotator cuff tendons, with the supraspinatus tendon being the most
L149@P4: common location for such deposition. These patients may either be asymptomatic or have
L150@P4: course of clinical onset that is similar to adhesive capsulitis in those with chronic non-severe
L151@P4: pain. It can also present as acute severe onset of atraumatic shoulder pain, an unusual
L152@P4: presentation for rotator cuff syndromes. The risk factors, evaluation, diagnosis, and some
L153@P4: treatments tend to be similar to rotator cuff tendinopathies although there are some
L154@P4: specific differences.
L155@P4: Degenerative Joint Disease (including Osteoarthrosis)
L156@P4: Degenerative joint disease is a term which includes any age-related changes in any joint
L157@P4: from any cause. Joints in the body are typically synovial fluid-filled, synovium-lined,
L158@P4: ligamentously encapsulated joints that allow for low friction movement between adjacent
L159@P4: bones. Common causes are osteoarthrosis, inflammatory disorders (e.g., rheumatoid
L160@P4: arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and psoriasis) and crystalline arthropathies (e.g.,
L161@P4: gout, pseudogout, apatites). Osteoarthrosis (OA) is the more precise name for
L162@P4: osteoarthritis, as there is no overt inflammation with redness, swelling, or palpable warmth.
L163@P4: OA, a degenerative disorder in the joint which primarily affects the cartilage on the articular
L164@P4: surface, is marked by thinning of that cartilage, osteophyte formation, and subchondral
L165@P4: sclerosis. Pain on movement and stiffness develop. Post-traumatic OA may develop in a
L166@P4: joint after a significant injury (e.g., fracture), in which case it is often delayed by many years.
L167@P4: If there is asymmetric disease in the shoulders on x-ray and this injury was occupational,
L168@P4: then the subsequent osteoarthrosis is also typically considered, at least in part,
L169@P4: occupational. As inflammatory and crystalline arthropathies are non-occupational, they are
L170@P4: not included in detail in this guideline.
L171@P4: Dislocation (Glenohumeral)
L172@P4: Shoulder dislocation occurs when a supramaximal force is applied to the shoulder
L173@P4: musculature and joint capsule, which definitionally are unable to resist, resulting in
L174@P4: stretching and partial rupture of the joint capsule. Labral tearing also usually accompanies
L175@P4: this injury. As this injury involves disrupting ligaments, it is technically a sprain. Frequently,
L176@P4: the shoulder will require a closed reduction by a medical professional, although sometimes
L177@P4: the patient accomplishes this prior to seeking medical care. Once dislocated, the shoulder
L178@P4: ligaments will become more lax, generating more instability and potentially causing
L179@P4: recurrent dislocations. Older patients with dislocations frequently have associated rotator
L180@P4-5: cuff tears and fractures. Copyright ©2025 Reed Group, LLC 6
