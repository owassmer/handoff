On November 17, 2025, the Industrial Ethyl Alcohol Exemption became a permanent subsection in the California Code of Regulations through the regular rulemaking process.This exemption amends section 66261.6 of title 22 of the California Code of Regulations by adopting an existing federal recycling exemption for industrial ethyl alcohol in 40 Code of Federal Regulations, section 261.6(a)(3)(i).Promulgated in 1985, this exemption was intended to reduce redundancy with existing distillery regulations established by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms (now the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau or TTB), and it initially applied to “spent” industrial ethyl alcohol alone.
In an effort to prevent additional fires and discourage illegal dumping incidents, DTSC has adopted the “Industrial Ethyl Alcohol Exemption.”

## Regulation Overview

The Industrial Ethyl Alcohol Exemption was first adopted as a temporary emergency rulemaking on October 7, 2024. It was then readopted twice, with the first readoption taking effect on April 7, 2025, and the second on July 8, 2025. On November 17, 2025, the Industrial Ethyl Alcohol Exemption became a permanent subsection in the California Code of Regulations through the regular rulemaking process.

This exemption amends section 66261.6 of title 22 of the California Code of Regulations by adopting an existing federal recycling exemption for industrial ethyl alcohol in 40 Code of Federal Regulations, section 261.6(a)(3)(i). Promulgated in 1985, this exemption was intended to reduce redundancy with existing distillery regulations established by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms (now the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau or TTB), and it initially applied to “spent” industrial ethyl alcohol alone. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Regulations adopted from the HSC are found in the California Code of Regulations, division 4.5, title 22 (22 CCR). In California the individual generating the waste must determine if the waste generated meets the criteria of hazardous waste as defined in 22 CCR section 66261.3.

In California, federally regulated hazardous waste is referred to as RCRA hazardous waste and is subject to 40 CFR, HSC, and 22 CCR. California-only hazardous waste is referred to as non-RCRA hazardous waste, which is subject to HSC and 22 CCR. A waste is a hazardous waste if it is either listed as a hazardous waste or if it demonstrates a characteristic of hazardous waste.

### Listed Wastes

In California, there are five types of listed wastes. These five listed wastes are organized into four categories:

Image

#### The F-list (non-specific source wastes)

This list identifies wastes from many common manufacturing and industrial processes, such as solvents that have been used for cleaning or degreasing. Since the processes producing these wastes occur in many different industry sectors, the F-listed wastes are known as wastes from non-specific sources. “Non-specific” means that, for the most part, they don’t come from one specific industry or one specific industrial or manufacturing process. The F-list appears in the hazardous waste regulations in 22 CCR section 66261.31.

#### Image

#### The P-list and the U-list (discarded commercial chemical products)

These lists include specific commercial chemical products that have not been used, but that will be (or have been) discarded. Industrial chemicals, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals are example of commercial chemical products that appear on these lists and become hazardous waste when discarded. P-listed wastes are Acute Hazardous Wastes, which are regulated more stringently because they are dangerous in smaller quantities than other hazardous wastes. The P- and U-lists appear in the hazardous waste regulations in 22 CCR sections 66261.33(e) and (f).

#### Image

#### The K-list (source-specific wastes)

This list includes certain wastes from specific industries, such as petroleum refining or pesticide manufacturing. Also, certain sludges and wastewaters from treatment and production processes in these specific industries are examples of source-specific wastes. The K-list appears in the hazardous waste regulations in 22 CCR section 66261.32.

#### Image

#### California M-listed Wastes (discarded mercury-containing products)

The list, found in 22 CCR section 66261.50, includes the following mercury (Hg) containing wastes:

  * M001: Mercury light switches in cars and cars that contain such switches prior to crushing, baling, shredding, or shearing
  * M002: Other mercury switches in products, including appliances
  * M003: Mercury-containing lamps and products with lamps except LCDs with Mercury back lighting
  * M004: Mercury-added novelties

DTSC’s mercury web page provides additional information regarding all types of mercury wastes.

According to HSC section 25250.1, “used oil” includes any oil that has been refined from crude oil, or any synthetic oil, that has been used and, as a result of use, or as a consequence of extended storage, or spillage, has been contaminated with physical or chemical impurities. In addition, used oil means a material that is subject to regulation as used oil under 40 CFR Part 279.

### Mixture & Derived-From Rules

The mixture & derived-from rules are intended to ensure that mixtures and residuals containing hazardous wastes are regulated in a manner that is protective of human health and the environment. It is the generator’s responsibility to determine if the mixture rule or derived-from rule applies when evaluating materials that are mixtures or that are residuals resulting from processing hazardous wastes. The hazardous waste mixture and derived-from rules are located in 22 CCR section 66261.3. As an example, a waste mixture or residual derived from either a characteristic waste or a California M-Listed waste is a hazardous waste only if it exhibits a characteristic of a hazardous waste (22 CCR sections 66261.3(b)(4) and 66261.3(c)(5)). On the other hand, in most cases, waste mixtures and treatment residuals involving F-, K-, P-, or U-listed wastes retain their listing and hazardous waste status, even if they do not exhibit a hazardous characteristic (subsections (a)(2)(E) and (F), (c), and (d)(2) of 22 CCR section 66261.3). There are also additional mixture rules specifically for mining wastes and for used oil.

#### Hazardous Waste Exclusions and Exemptions

There are exclusions and exemptions for certain materials or wastes. The first step of the waste determination process (required by generators in 22 CCR section 66262.11) is to determine if the material is excluded from regulation as either a waste or hazardous waste as provided in 22 CCR section 66261.4 and HSC section 25143.2). Additional exclusions, as well as exemptions from various requirements for certain hazardous wastes, are found in 22 CCR section 66261.6, HSC sections 25140 through 25145.4, HSC section 25124, and other laws and regulations. DTSC has provided a self-paced internet course on hazardous waste identification to further assist in properly identifying waste.

#### Hazardous Waste Recycling

According to California law, recycling is one of the ways that a material is considered to be discarded and therefore a waste unless excluded (refer to HSC section 25124). Some materials are conditionally excluded from being regulated as wastes when recycled in certain ways, as specified in HSC section 25143.2. There are also some recycling-related exclusions in 22 CCR section CCR 66261.4, and several exemptions for recycling activities in 22 CCR section 66261.6.

#### Test Methods

Sampling and analysis of materials and wastes, for hazardous waste identification purposes, utilize U.S. EPA’s publication: “Test Methods for Evaluating Solid Waste, Physical/Chemical Methods,” SW-846, commonly referred to just as “SW-846.”

In California the test methods referenced in 22 CCR section 66261.20 are SW-846, 3^{rd} Edition.

##### 1. Federal Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)

Toxic as defined through application of a laboratory test procedure called the Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure, (TCLP). The TCLP identifies wastes (as hazardous) that may leach hazardous concentrations of toxic substances into the environment when disposed. The result of the TCLP test is compared to the Regulatory Level (RL) in the table in 22 CCR section 66261.24(a)(1). This criterion does not apply to wastes that are excluded from regulation under the RCRA.

##### 2. California Total Threshold Limit Concentration (TTLC) and Waste Extraction Test (WET)

Toxic as defined through application of laboratory test procedures called the “total digestion” and the WET, located in 22 CCR section 66261.126, Appendix II. The total digestion breaks down the waste into its component chemicals, which are then analyzed to reveal the total concentration of each toxic chemical in the waste. The total concentration of each toxic chemical is then compared to an RL called the Total Threshold Limit Concentration (TTLC). The WET is a method that is very similar to the TCLP, extracting the soluble toxic chemicals that would leach in a landfill setting. Those extracts are analyzed, and the concentration of each toxic chemical is compared to its Soluble Threshold Limit Concentration (STLC). A waste with any toxic chemical concentration equaling or exceeding its TTLC or STLC is identified as hazardous. The TTLCs and STLCs for regulated toxic chemicals are found in 22 CCR section 66261.24(a)(2).

##### 3. Acute Oral Toxicity

The waste is toxic if ingested. As stated in 22 CCR section 66261.24(a)(3), a waste is identified as being toxic if it has an acute oral LD_{50} less than 2,500 mg/kg. A calculated oral LD_{50}, as specified in 22 CCR section 66261.24(c),_{ }may be used. “Acute oral LD_{50}” means the dose of a substance or mixture of substances, in milligrams per kilogram of test animal body weight, which, when administered orally as a single dose, produces death within 14 days in half of a group of 10 or more laboratory white rats which have fasted for 24 hours immediately prior to administration of the dose, and which weigh between 200 and 300 grams each, as defined in 22 CCR section 66260.10.
