## C1. After AB 2801, does a good-faith but late or defective statement still let the landlord keep supported charges?

**Short answer.** Yes, in substance, but not by keeping them automatically. Under (h)(7), losing the whole deposit requires bad faith. A landlord whose statement is late or incomplete in good faith keeps the right to recover proven, reasonable charges. *Granberry* treats that as a setoff or claim the landlord must prove in a later judicial proceeding, not the summary right to retain. **Strength: strong** that good-faith noncompliance does not trigger (h)(7). **Moderate** on how the recovery works in practice, because no post-AB 2801 appellate case exists.

**Authorities**
- **Civ 1950.5(h)(7)** (AB 2801, Stats. 2024 ch. 280; carried forward by AB 414): "The landlord shall not be entitled to claim any amount of the security if the landlord, in bad faith, fails to comply with this subdivision." The forfeiture covers the security, and its condition is bad faith (`../texts/CA_CIV/1950.5.txt`).
- ***Granberry v. Islay Investments* (1995) 9 Cal.4th 738** (Supreme Court; Stanford mirror, `CASE_Granberry_v_Islay_Investments_1995_mirror.txt`).
  - **Holding** (pp. 749–750): a landlord that "has failed in good faith" to use the deduct-and-retain procedure may recover unpaid rent, repair and cleaning damages in a later judicial proceeding, by setoff or otherwise. It must prove the damages by a preponderance and that the amount is reasonable, and the tenant may raise laches, unclean hands or estoppel.
  - **Reasoning** (pp. 747–748): courts will not impose penalties beyond those the statute provides expressly or by necessary implication. Because the statute penalized only bad-faith retention, the Legislature did not intend to bar good-faith landlords from setoff.
  - **The summary right is still lost** (p. 745): without a timely accounting, "the right to retain ... has not been perfected, and he must return the entire deposit."
  - **Fn. 6** (dictum, deliberately open): "we ... express no opinion regarding the rights of landlords who have acted in bad faith."
  - Later history: the Scholar check found no published case resolving fn. 6 or retreating from the holding. The citing cases use *Granberry* on setoff, statutory construction and unpassed bills (for example *Fassberg*, 2007; *Pacific Fertility Cases*, 2022; *Coziahr v. Otay Water*, 2024).
- ***250 L.L.C. v. PhotoPoint Corp.* (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 703** (1st Dist.; commercial deposit statute, Civ 1950.7; Justia mirror). It reads *Granberry* to mean a good-faith violator "may offset against its damages only those amounts which it properly could have claimed of the security deposit in the first place." Persuasive by analogy, and it is the case the AB 2801 committee analysis cites.
- **AB 2801 legislative history** (official, saved).
  - **Senate Judiciary analysis** (June 11, 2024, April 25 version), p. 11. The bad-faith bar "does not require strict adherence; just a good faith attempt to comply. Thus, if a landlord believes they are complying with the requirements in good faith, they may still be able to withhold or deduct ... for allowable charges." After a good-faith violation, the landlord "may offset their damages by any amounts which they could have properly deducted" (citing *PhotoPoint*).
  - **Assembly floor analysis** (concurrence, Aug. 2024). The final Senate amendments were chaptering-only, so the Senate analysis describes the enacted (h)(7).
  - **Legislative Counsel's Digest** (chaptered). Its wording is broader than the text: "prohibit the landlord from making a claim against the tenant or the security if the landlord, in bad faith, fails to comply." Where the two differ, courts follow the text, but the Digest gives a tenant an argument that a bad-faith landlord also loses claims against the tenant.
  - **Section 1** (intent): "landlords do not subsidize improvements to their rental properties with a former tenant's security deposit."
- ***Brooks v. Greystar*, ECF 56** (S.D. Cal. Aug. 7, 2025; GovInfo, official). It quotes (h)(7) and *Granberry* p. 745 at the pleading stage to say complete relief (return of deposits) is available. It does not construe bad faith.
- **Since 2024.** No appellate decision construing (h)(7) was located: Scholar returned "no match" for "subdivision (h)(7)", domain-limited web searches found only statute copies, and the *Granberry* cited-by list was read. An unpublished June 2026 decision applying former 1950.5 (*SM 10000 Property v. Pecci*, B337813) was read and is irrelevant.

**Remaining disagreement**
1. **Bad-faith landlords' separate claims.** *Granberry* fn. 6 left open whether a bad-faith landlord can still sue for damages beyond the deposit. (h)(7)'s text reaches only "the security"; the Digest says "claim against the tenant or the security." This is unresolved.
2. **Scope of the forfeiture.** "Any amount of the security" reads as the whole deposit, even if the bad-faith failure concerns one line.
3. **Retaining after a missed deadline.** In *Granberry* the landlords kept the money and later won setoff, but the jury had found they honestly believed it was rent. A landlord that knows it missed day 21 and keeps the deposit anyway faces a closer bad-faith question (C2).

**What it means for Handoff**
- Timeliness and completeness on day 21 remain the real protection. A complete, timely statement gives the summary right to retain. Anything less turns each kept charge into a setoff the operator must prove in a dispute, with interest on the deposit and equitable defenses available to the tenant.
- When a defect is found after sending (a missing invoice or photograph, a wrong figure), cure it at once (send the document or a corrected statement) and record when it was found. Bad faith turns on knowledge (C2), so a defect left uncured after discovery is the main route from good faith to forfeiture.
- In each kept line's exposure, show three separate states: perfected (timely and complete); unperfected but defensible (a good-faith defect, so the line is recoverable only by proof as a setoff); and at risk of bad faith (full deposit plus up to twice the deposit).
- Owen's ruling is needed on the missed-deadline branch (see the end).

## C2. When do statutory damages of up to twice the deposit apply under 1950.5(m), and what is "bad faith"?

**Short answer**
- Statutory damages require two things: a claim or retention of the security that violates 1950.5, made in bad faith. Even then the award is discretionary ("may subject ... up to twice"; "may award ... whenever the facts warrant").
- No published California decision defines "bad faith" for 1950.5. The best available indication is a subjective standard: keeping money while knowing, or not honestly believing, that it was authorized. Negligence and honest mistakes do not qualify.
- An isolated documentation failure made in good faith is not enough. A knowing or systematic practice of withholding required documents, charging flat fees, or keeping money after learning a requirement was missed can support bad faith. It would then also trigger the whole-deposit forfeiture in (h)(7).

**Strength: moderate.** The definition rests on a superseded opinion, the Supreme Court's acceptance of a no-bad-faith verdict, and legislative history, not on a binding definition.

**Authorities**
- **Civ 1950.5(m)** (text). It reaches "bad faith claim or retention ... in violation of this section." The landlord bears the burden on the reasonableness of the amounts. The court may award statutory damages on its own motion.
- ***Granberry v. Islay Investments* (Cal. Ct. App. 1993)**, superseded by the grant of review (FindLaw mirror; **not citable**; `CASE_Granberry_CoA_1993_superseded_NOT_CITABLE_mirror.txt`).
  - It approved a jury instruction defining bad faith as "not prompted by an honest mistake, but by some interested or sinister motive, not simply bad judgment or negligence, but rather the conscious doing of a wrong."
  - It held that bad faith "required a finding that what was done was done with the belief that it was not authorized by statute," reasoning by analogy to the subjective test under CCP 128.5.
  - The Supreme Court did not review the instruction ruling (9 Cal.4th at p. 743, fn. 4) and itself relied on the jury's no-bad-faith finding (fn. 6).
- ***Peviani v. Arbors at California Oaks Property Owner, LLC* (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 874** (4th Dist., Div. 2; published; Justia mirror).
  - Liability for wrongful retention needs only a paid deposit, a residential dwelling and deductions that were not reasonably necessary. Bad faith is needed only for the statutory ("punitive") damages and can be decided after liability.
  - It reversed the denial of class certification for a deposit class. Plaintiffs' theory was that widespread unsupported charges and missing documentation could let a jury find bad faith; the court did not decide that.
- ***Brooks v. Greystar*, ECF 48** (S.D. Cal. Sept. 26, 2024; mirror). Allegations that a manager "in bad faith" systematically withheld documentation, charged flat cleaning fees and paid late stated 1950.5 and UCL claims. Pleading stage only.
- **AB 2801 Senate Judiciary analysis.** For (h)(7), "a good faith attempt to comply" is enough.
- **Cases the brief named**
  - *Orozco v. Casimiro* (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th Supp. 7 (L.A. App. Div.; LawLink mirror) exists, but it is not a deposit bad-faith case. It holds a flat $50 late fee in a residential lease void under Civ 1671(d), because the landlord neither pleaded nor proved that damages were impracticable to fix. That matters for the charge schedule (Owen has already excluded flat fees), not for bad faith.
  - *Ingalls v. Bell* and *Kovarik v. DeVaul Properties* were not found by name searches (recorded). Their existence is unconfirmed and they are not relied on.

**Is a documentation failure enough?**
- **Text.** Retaining after an (h)(2) documentation failure is a retention "in violation of this section." Whether it is in bad faith depends on state of mind.
- **Honest omission.** Under the subjective standard, an honest omission such as a misfiled invoice is not bad faith.
- **Professional managers.** A practice of never sending invoices or photographs is hard to present as honest mistake, because the requirements are written into the statute and the manager's own procedures. *Brooks* and *Peviani* show plaintiffs framing exactly that inference.
- **Improper charges.** Knowingly charging items 1950.5 forbids (ordinary wear and tear, conditions present at move-in, upgrades, flat fees) is a bad-faith "claim," whatever the paperwork.

**Remaining disagreement.** Trial courts may apply a looser objective or "reckless disregard" test; practitioner sources say so, but no appellate authority supports it. Whether statutory damages are assessed per deposit or per tenant, and how they combine with class or UCL relief, has no 1950.5-specific appellate answer.

**What it means for Handoff**
- Handoff's process is the operator's good-faith evidence: timestamped photographs, invoices or hours and rate, a recorded responsibility finding and the reason for each line, the estimate route under (h)(3), documents sent within 14 days of a request, and cure on discovery. Keep that record per line.
- Exposure per account, if bad faith is found: the whole deposit, plus up to twice the deposit, plus actual damages and interest. Brooks- and Peviani-style aggregation is the portfolio risk of a systematic defect.
- Never send a statement Handoff knows is missing a required element without either curing it or using the statutory estimate route.

## C4. The manager as the owner's agent

**Short answer**
- The owner, as landlord, is liable for 1950.5 violations its manager commits within the agency.
- The manager is exposed in its own right. California agency law makes an agent personally liable for acts "wrongful in their nature." Federal courts have let 1950.5 and UCL claims proceed against managers that controlled deposit administration, including UCL restitution of money the owners received.
- Whether a manager is itself a "landlord" liable for (m) statutory damages has no appellate answer.
- In small claims, the manager may appear for the owner under CCP 116.540(h). Handoff may not.

**Strength:** strong on owner liability and small-claims appearance; **moderate** on manager exposure (federal district orders plus statute); **open** on the manager's statutory damages.

**Authorities**
- **Civ 1950.5.** The duties run to "the landlord," which is undefined. (f)(1) and (i) speak of "the landlord or the landlord's agent." Statutory damages in (m) run against "the landlord or the landlord's successors in interest."
- **Agency statutes** (official, saved).
  - Civ 2330: an agent's acts within its authority bind the principal.
  - Civ 2338: a principal is responsible for the agent's wrongful acts "in and as a part of the transaction of such business."
  - Civ 2343(3): an agent is personally responsible to third persons "when his acts are wrongful in their nature."
- ***Brooks v. Greystar*, ECF 48** (Sept. 26, 2024; mirror) and **ECF 56** (Aug. 7, 2025; GovInfo, official). S.D. Cal., nonbinding.
  - Claims against Greystar as manager of owner-entity properties survived dismissal where Greystar allegedly controlled deposit administration (ECF 48).
  - The owner entities were indispensable only for relief extinguishing balances owed to them, because "Greystar cannot give Plaintiffs something that does not belong to them" (ECF 48).
  - Return of deposits is relief the manager can provide (ECF 56).
  - Later history: the case is in putative-class discovery as of September 2026. The Ninth Circuit denied a Rule 23(f) petition on Dec. 17, 2025. There is no class ruling yet.
- ***Leaser v. Prime Ascot, L.P.*, ECF 60** (E.D. Cal. June 12, 2024; GovInfo, official; cited as 2024 WL 3011226). A manager that "orchestrated and engaged in" excessive-fee and deposit practices "may be held liable in restitution" under the UCL even if the money went to the owners; any indemnity is between manager and owners. It relies on published California cases on UCL restitution from non-recipients (*Sarpas*, *Troyk*, *Shersher*). Nonbinding; no later merits ruling appears in the GovInfo package.
- ***Peviani*** (2021, published). The owner LLC and its manager (JRK Residential) were sued together and the deposit class certification denial was reversed. The opinion does not separately analyse the manager's liability.
- **CCP 116.540(h), (j)** (official, `../texts/CA_CCP/116.540.txt`).
  - An owner of rental property may appear "through a property agent under contract with the owner to manage the rental of that property." The agent must have been retained principally to manage, not to appear in small claims, and the claim must relate to the property.
  - The representative files a declaration of authority and its basis.
  - A corporate party otherwise appears only through a regular employee or officer (116.540(b)).
  - Small claims has no case law on point, and none is needed.

**Remaining disagreement.** Whether a manager is a "landlord" for (h)(7) and (m), or liable only through Civ 2343 and the UCL (restitution, not statutory damages). The federal orders did not need to decide it.

**What it means for Handoff**
- **Who the statement comes from.** The duty is the landlord's, and an agent may perform it (Civ 2330). The statement should name the owner entity as landlord and the party to whom any balance is owed, and be issued by the manager as its agent. Handoff transmits it under the operator's name as the manager's service provider, which matches Owen's Oct. 3 agreement. Handoff should not appear as a party or as a collector (see the debt-collector section).
- **Acceptance.** Because the manager carries its own exposure, the operator's acceptance of the account is the manager's act. Record it that way.
- **Small claims.**
  - **Owner as defendant.** In a tenant's deposit claim against the owner, the manager's employee appears with the 116.540(j) declaration. If the tenant also names the manager, the manager appears through its own employee or officer.
  - **Handoff staff** cannot appear for either.
  - **Owner as plaintiff.** The owner, appearing through the manager, is subject to the $6,250 entity limit and the two-claims-over-$2,500 rule (MAP DP7.6, DP8.9).
- **Indemnity.** Who bears what between owner and manager is a matter for the management agreement, which Handoff should read when onboarding an operator.

## C10. The limitation period for 1950.5(m) statutory damages

**Short answer.** Probably not one year. Statutory damages under (m) are discretionary: the court "may" award them, "up to" twice the deposit. California appellate courts apply the one-year penalty statute (CCP 340(a)) only to mandatory penalties. On that line, (m) damages take either the three-year period for statutory liabilities (CCP 338(a)) or the underlying claim's period, four years on a written lease (CCP 337). No appellate decision addresses 1950.5(m) itself. **Strength: moderate** against one year; **unsettled** between three and four years.

**Authorities** (all published, all saved; mirrors labelled)
- ***Holland v. Nelson* (1970) 5 Cal.App.3d 308** (1st Dist.). Discretionary treble damages under the Dance Act are not a "penalty or forfeiture," so CCP 338 (three years) applies. The contrary construction "would put a plaintiff in the position of being unable to determine which statute of limitations applies to his cause until, after trial, the court determined in its discretion whether to allow treble damages."
- ***Menefee v. Ostawari* (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 239** (1st Dist.; a landlord–tenant case). Mandatory treble damages under the San Francisco rent ordinance are a penalty, so one year. It restates *Holland*: where the court has a "discretionary option" to award multiple damages, CCP 340(1) does not govern.
- ***Jensen v. BMW of North America* (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 133** (3d Dist.). This is the closest analogue: a discretionary civil penalty of up to two times actual damages for a willful violation. It is "governed by the four-year limitations period" of the underlying claim; "section 340, subdivision (1), applies only where the penalty is mandatory." Followed in *Hypertouch* (2011) and restated in *Shamsian v. ARCO* (2003; snippet only, not saved).
- ***Prudential Home Mortgage Co. v. Superior Court* (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1236** (4th Dist., Div. 3, the Orange County division). A mandatory $300 forfeiture is a penalty, so one year. It restates the "settled rule" that double or treble damages beyond actual loss are penal. This is the strongest language for one year, but the statute there was mandatory.
- ***Hypertouch, Inc. v. ValueClick* (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 805** (2d Dist.). Liquidated damages that must be awarded once claimed and proven are a penalty even though the amount is discretionary (following *TJX Companies*, 2008). It reaffirms *Jensen* and *Holland* for awards the court may decline altogether.
- **Language pointing toward "penalty"**
  - *Granberry* calls the bad-faith remedy a "penalty."
  - *Peviani* calls the (l)/(m) damages "punitive."
  - The superseded *Granberry* Court of Appeal opinion says bad faith "authorized a penalty."
  - None of these addresses limitation periods, and *Menefee* warns that "penal" damages are not the same as punitive damages.
- **Related periods (context)**
  - Deposit refund: CCP 337 (written lease, four years) or 339 (oral, two years), per the DRE guide.
  - UCL restitution: four years (B&P 17208). *Brooks* ECF 48 applied it to the class period and discussed class-action tolling.

**Application.** (m) says the bad-faith violator "may" be subject to damages "up to" twice the deposit, and the court "may award" them "whenever the facts warrant." A court may therefore find bad faith and still award nothing. That is discretion over whether to award, not merely over the amount, so it is the *Holland* and *Jensen* category, not the *Prudential* and *Hypertouch* one.

**Remaining disagreement.** A tenant suing after one year will meet a 340(a) defense built on *Prudential*'s "settled rule" and *Granberry*'s "penalty" language. The choice between 338(a) and 337 depends on whether the court treats the deposit claim as statutory or contractual (*Jensen* followed the underlying claim's period).

**What it means for Handoff.** Treat statutory-damages exposure as live for at least three years after vacating, and plan for four. Do not discount it in a settlement just because a year has passed. Keep account records at least four years after vacating, plus any small-claims appeal period and class-tolling margin (MAP DP9.3 already says four years).

## Debt-collector status of the manager, Handoff and the collector

This section takes the collection research of Oct. 1 as its base (OPERATING_BRANCHES C01, C02, C18; `tenancy/FINDINGS.md`; `resolution_tenancy/FINDINGS.md`; `resolution_review/DEPENDENCY_AUDIT.md` section 3). It re-checks that work, adds the Ninth Circuit grounding that `federal.md` section 2 asked for, and adds 2025–2026 material on licensing.

### 1. FDCPA (federal), Ninth Circuit

**Short answer**
- **The manager** is not an FDCPA "debt collector" for a departing tenant's balance when it obtained the account before default. That is the *De Dios* holding. **Strong.**
- **Handoff**, engaged under a standing agreement before move-out, very likely fits the same "not in default when obtained" exclusion for the accounts it runs from move-out onward. Its principal purpose is also not debt collection. **Moderate to strong.** No case addresses a software or service vendor to a manager, and *Romine* shows a vendor can collect "indirectly."
- **The outside collector** that takes a balance after default is a debt collector, and is strictly liable for amounts the landlord supplied without authority (*Reichert*). **Strong.**

**Authorities** (saved; official Ninth Circuit and Supreme Court PDFs unless noted)
- **15 U.S.C. 1692a(6).** A debt collector is any business whose principal purpose is collecting debts, or anyone who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due ... another." It excludes (F)(ii) a debt the person originated and (F)(iii) "a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person." A creditor collecting its own debts becomes a debt collector if it "uses any name other than his own which would indicate that a third person is collecting."
- ***De Dios v. International Realty & Investments*, 641 F.3d 1071 (9th Cir. 2011).**
  - **Holding:** a residential property manager "is exempt from the definition of a 'debt collector' under § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) because it obtained the right to collect [the] rent before the debt was contractually overdue and before it triggered applicable contractual conditions or state law governing default."
  - Default is judged by the underlying contract and governing law, as of when the collector obtained the debt, not when it sent the letter (fn. 2).
  - The court expressly declined to decide whether a debt servicer meets the primary definition at all.
  - Later history: no later published Ninth Circuit decision revisiting it was located. *Yatooma* (below) applies it.
- ***Reichert v. National Credit Systems*, 531 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2008).** A collection agency collecting a former tenant's apartment balance violated 1692f(1) by adding a $225 attorney fee the lease did not authorize. The landlord's history of accurate referrals and a conclusory affidavit did not establish the bona fide error defense. Collector status was not disputed. This confirms that apartment balances are FDCPA "debts" and that the collector must validate each component.
- ***McAdory v. M.N.S. & Associates*, 952 F.3d 1089 (9th Cir. 2020)** and ***Henson v. Santander*, 582 U.S. 79 (2017).** Owning the debt is not a complete exclusion; a business whose principal purpose is collection can be a debt collector even if it outsources contact. These matter only if an owner sells balances.
- ***Romine v. Diversified Collection Services*, 155 F.3d 1142 (9th Cir. 1998)** (law.resource.org mirror). Western Union's telegram service for collectors, which contacted debtors, obtained their numbers and relayed collection messages, "go[es] beyond mere information gathering or message delivery." It could make Western Union a debt collector that collects "indirectly." A vendor's status therefore turns on the exclusions, not on calling itself a messenger or software.
- ***Yatooma v. OP Property Management* (C.D. Cal. July 20, 2017)** (persuasive; Justia mirror). The owner and the manager were not debt collectors: property management, not collection, was their primary function, and they originated the lease ((F)(ii), citing *De Dios*).

**Limits and traps**
- *De Dios* protects only accounts obtained before default. Accounts Handoff takes over that are already in default, such as a new operator's backlog of former-tenant balances or an account another system already treated as delinquent, fall outside (F)(iii). For those, Handoff may be an FDCPA debt collector. It would then need to use its own true name (1692e(14)) and give the Regulation F validation notice.
- Under *De Dios*, "default" is set by the lease and state law. A departing tenant's balance is not due, so not in default, until the statement's payment date passes.
- If communications are styled to suggest a third party is collecting (a separate "recovery department" or a collector-like brand), the creditor and those acting for it can lose their status as creditor (1692a(6), false name) and Handoff can face flat-rating liability under 1692j.

### 2. Rosenthal Act (Civ 1788.2, as amended by AB 1521, effective Jan. 1, 2026)

**Short answer**
- Rosenthal covers anyone who "regularly, on behalf of that person or others, engages in debt collection," expressly including anyone who "composes and sells ... letters ... used ... for debt collection." It has no not-in-default exclusion; *Davidson* refuses to import the federal limit.
- It reaches only "consumer debt" arising from a "consumer credit transaction."
- Prepaid rent that goes unpaid is not credit. Postpaid utilities are credit. Damage charges follow the underlying transaction.
- So for a balance that includes postpaid utilities, the manager and probably Handoff are Rosenthal "debt collectors" for that component, and Rosenthal's conduct rules apply to the whole communication that seeks it.
- **Strength:** strong for postpaid utilities (published *Hagey*, decided by the Orange County division); moderate for advance rent (persuasive federal orders only, now echoed by a 2026 legislative committee analysis); unchanged since the Oct. 1 findings.

**Authorities**
- **Civ 1788.2(b), (c), (e), (f)** (official text in `../texts/CA_CIV/1788.2.txt`).
- ***Hagey v. Solar Service Experts* (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 1303** (4th Dist., Div. 3, G061836; published). Monthly billing for electricity already supplied is a consumer credit transaction.
- ***Davidson v. Seterus* (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 283** (published). "Credit" means acquiring something without immediate payment on a promise to pay later. Rosenthal's collector definition is broader than the FDCPA's and has no default-timing limit.
- ***Olson v. La Jolla Neurological Associates* (2022) 85 Cal.App.5th 723** (published). What counts is actual participation in, and control of, collection, not job title.
- These three were captured on Oct. 1 and are copied into `authorities/` with their original headers.
- **Assembly Banking and Finance analysis of SB 546** (June 2026; official). Committee staff: "rental payments, which are made at the beginning of the term of occupancy, are not subject to the RFDCPA because it is not paid on credit. However, utilities, which are paid for after usage, are considered 'on credit' and therefore subject to the RFDCPA" (citing *Davidson*). This is legislative staff analysis, not law, but it is current and matches the Oct. 1 findings.
- **New searches** (California courts since 2023; all courts since 2023; web) found no new appellate holding on rent and Rosenthal.

### 3. Debt Collection Licensing Act (Fin 100000 et seq.)

**Short answer**
- A license is required to "engage in the business of debt collection in this state" (100001(a)). The definitions copy Rosenthal, so only consumer-credit debt counts (100002(e), (f), (j)).
- **The manager.** A person licensed under the Real Estate Law is exempt from licensing (100001(b)(1)), though not from Rosenthal conduct rules. A DRE-licensed manager is therefore exempt (confirm the actual license).
- **Handoff** has no exemption of its own. Whether it needs a license turns on whether composing and sending balance demands that include postpaid utility charges, for many managers, is "the business of debt collection." **Unsettled; real risk.** The one directly analogous actor, a third-party utility and rent billing agent (Conservice), holds a DCLA license, and the 2026 bill to exempt such billing agents did not pass.

**Authorities**
- **Fin 100001, 100002** (official text in `../texts/CA_FIN/`).

**Remaining disagreement**
- Is a lease's utility charge "residential rental debt" (outside the DCLA under the draft) or postpaid consumer credit (inside, per *Hagey* and the 2026 committee analysis)?
- Is Handoff's collection activity "incidental" to its turnover and account business?
- Does an itemized deposit statement that applies the deposit to utilities, with no payment demand, count as "debt collection" at all? Applying the security is not asking the tenant to pay. A statement showing a balance due and asking for payment is collection.

### 4. What each answer means for how Handoff phrases and sends balance communications

1. **Name and voice.** Every statement and balance communication goes out in the operator's name. It identifies the owner entity as landlord and creditor and the manager as its agent, and is sent through Handoff as the manager's service provider. Never use a name or format suggesting a third-party collector (FDCPA false-name rule; 1692j). The tenant page carries the same identity.
2. **Tag each balance component.** Prepaid rent and fees under it: not credit. Postpaid utilities: credit. Damage and cleaning: by the underlying bargain, normally not credit. Payment plans: no new credit unless there is a new advance (Oct. 1 resolution). Any communication seeking a credit component is Rosenthal-covered conduct for the whole communication.
3. **Collector-grade content for every balance demand.** These are cheap to meet and remove the coverage question for the creditor side.
   - Only amounts the lease or law authorizes (1692f(1); *Reichert*; MAP C12).
   - An itemized basis for each line.
   - No false urgency or threats.
   - No misstatement of legal status or consequences.
   - Honoured disputes and requests for documents.
   - Communication limits respected once the tenant disputes or asks Handoff to stop.
4. **The collector handoff.** Send the collector only validated components, with their evidence. Corrections and payments must reach it at once; *Reichert* makes the collector strictly liable, and the operator inherits indemnity exposure. The collector's own notices (Regulation F 1006.34, Civ 1812.700, 1788.14.5 where an assignment qualifies) are its duty, but Handoff should supply what they need (OPERATING_BRANCHES C04–C09).
5. **Accounts obtained after default.** When onboarding a customer's existing delinquent balances, either route them straight to the licensed collector or have Handoff act openly as an FDCPA debt collector for them (its own name, validation notice). Do not run them through the operator-name flow.
6. **Licensing.** Until Owen decides, keep Handoff from sending payment demands for postpaid utility balances in its own operating role beyond the 1950.5 statement. Leave follow-up demands for utility balances to the DRE-licensed manager's own staff or the licensed collector, or obtain a DCLA license first. The 1950.5 statement itself can carry utility deductions from the deposit (MAP DP5, C6, C7), with the reasoning above recorded.

## Points needing Owen's ruling

1. **Missed day 21 or a defect found late (C1).** When a statement goes out late or incomplete in good faith, should Handoff (a) keep the supported charges and defend them as setoff, with the exposure shown, or (b) refund the deposit and bill the charges as a balance? *Granberry* says the deposit "must" be returned once the summary right is lost but allows setoff; knowingly keeping the money raises the bad-faith risk.
2. **Cure rule (C1, C2).** A standing instruction that a known missing element (invoice, photograph, hours and rate) is cured within a set number of days of discovery, and that no statement goes out knowingly incomplete except through the (h)(3) estimate route.
3. **Statutory-damages tail (C10).** Whether exposure displays and settlement valuations assume three years or four, given that one year is unlikely.
4. **Statement identity (C4).** Confirm the format: the owner entity as landlord and creditor, the manager as agent sender, the operator's name on Handoff's emails. Also confirm whether the account recommendation should show the manager's own exposure (UCL restitution, possible statutory damages) separately from the owner's.
5. **DCLA path for Handoff.** Choose between (a) obtaining a DCLA license before Handoff sends utility-balance demands in California; (b) keeping utility-balance demands with the DRE-licensed manager's staff and the licensed collector; or (c) relying on the unsettled "incidental" and "residential rental debt" arguments, with the exposure stated. Recommendation: (a) or (b) for the demonstration's balance-owed variation.
6. **Already-delinquent balances at onboarding (FDCPA).** Route them directly to the collector, or let Handoff collect openly as an FDCPA debt collector.
7. **Default coverage of utilities (Rosenthal).** Confirm that postpaid utility components are treated as Rosenthal-covered by default.
