He timely appealed thereafter.4\nL75@P3: DISCUSSION\nL76@P3: The sole issue presented in this appeal hinges on statutory interpretation of\nL77@P3: certain provisions of the Rosenthal Act. Plaintiff argues the trial court erred in\nL78@P3: concluding the operative complaint does not state a cause of action under the statutory\nL79@P3: scheme because it fails to allege a consumer credit transaction, as that term is defined by\nL80@P3: statute. He contends the court overlooked a key aspect of the statutory definitions which\nL81@P3: encompasses debt alleged to be due or owing, not simply debt that is, in fact, due or\nL82@P3: owing. Defendant contends the court got it right—plaintiff did not acquire anything on\nL83@P3: credit and, therefore, was not party to a consumer credit transaction to which the statutory\nL84@P3: scheme applies. Plaintiff’s argument has merit.\nL85@P3: Principles of statutory interpretation and standard of review\nL86@P3: “The basic rules for statutory construction are well settled. [¶] . . . ‘[O]ur\nL87@P3: fundamental task . . . is to determine the Legislature’s intent so as to effectuate the law’s\nL88@P3: purpose.’ [Citation.] ‘We begin with the plain language of the statute, affording the\nL89@P3: words of the provision their ordinary and usual meaning and viewing them in their\nL90@P3: statutory context, because the language employed in the Legislature’s enactment\nL91@P3: generally is the most reliable indicator of legislative intent.’ [Citations.] The plain\nL92@P3: meaning controls if there is no ambiguity in the statutory language. [Citation.] If,\nL93@P3: however, ‘the statutory language may reasonably be given more than one interpretation,\nL94@P3: “‘“courts may consider various extrinsic aids, including the purpose of the statute, the\nL95@P3: evils to be remedied, the legislative history, public policy, and the statutory scheme\nL96@P3: encompassing the statute.””” [Citation.]” (People v. Cornett (2012) 53 Cal.4th 1261,\nL97@P3: 1265.)\nL98@P3: “[I]n reviewing a trial court’s order sustaining a demurrer without leave to\nL99@P3: amend[,] ‘[w]e independently review the ruling . . . and determine de novo whether the\nL100@P3-4: complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a cause of action. [Citation.] We assume the 5\nL101@P4: truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations, facts that reasonably can be inferred\nL102@P4: from those expressly pleaded, and matters of which judicial notice has been taken.\nL103@P4: [Citation.] We construe the pleading in a reasonable manner and read the allegations in\nL104@P4: context.’” (Davidson v. Seterus, Inc. (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 283, 294 (Davidson).)\nL105@P4: Collection of consumer debts\nL106@P4: Enacted in 1977, the Rosenthal Act aims “to prohibit debt collectors from\nL107@P4: engaging in unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the collection of consumer debts and\nL108@P4: to require debtors to act fairly in entering into and honoring such debts.” (§ 1788.1,\nL109@P4: subd. (b).) Among other matters, it generally requires “debt collector[s] collecting or\nL110@P4: attempting to collect a consumer debt” to comply with the provisions of its federal\nL111@P4: counterpart, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) (15 U.S.C. § 1692 et seq.).\nL112@P4: (§ 1788.17; Davidson, supra, 21 Cal.App.5th at p. 295.) The legislation is “‘a remedial\nL113@P4: statute [that] should be interpreted broadly in order to effectuate its purpose.’”\nL114@P4: (Komarova v. National Credit Acceptance, Inc. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 324, 340.)\nL115@P4: To determine whether defendant and its activities vis-à-vis plaintiff fall\nL116@P4: within its scope of the Rosenthal Act, we turn to the Act’s definitions. “Debt collector” is\nL117@P4: defined as “any person who, in the ordinary course of business, regularly, on behalf of\nL118@P4: that person or others, engages in debt collection.” (§ 1788.2, subd. (c).) “[D]ebt\nL119@P4: collection” consists of “any act or practice in connection with the collection of consumer\nL120@P4: debts.” (§ 1788.2, subd. (b).) In turn, “consumer debt” is defined to be “money,\nL121@P4: property, or their equivalent, due or owing or alleged to be due or owing from a natural\nL122@P4: person by reason of a consumer credit transaction.” (§ 1788.2, subd. (f).) A “consumer\nL123@P4: credit transaction” is “a transaction between a natural person and another person in which\nL124@P4: property, services, or money is acquired on credit by that natural person from the other\nL125@P4-5: person primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.” (§ 1788.2, subd. (e).)6\nL126@P5: Thus, for debt collection activity concerning money to fall within the scope\nL127@P5: of the Rosenthal Act, it must involve money due or owing, or alleged to be due or owing,\nL128@P5: by reason of a transaction in which property, services, or money is acquired on credit\nL129@P5: primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.\nL130@P5: The question before us is whether the operative complaint alleges activity\nL131@P5: by defendant which falls within the ambit of consumer debt collection activity. The\nL132@P5: simple answer is it does. To begin, the allegations state defendant sent plaintiff bills and\nL133@P5: late payment notices about money which defendant claimed plaintiff owed. This satisfies\nL134@P5: the money “alleged to be due or owing” component of the statutory scheme.\nL135@P5: Defendant incorrectly focuses on the apparently undisputed fact that\nL136@P5: plaintiff did not actually owe the money for which he was billed. The plain statutory\nL137@P5: language makes clear the Legislature did not choose to protect only those who owe\nL138@P5: money. (See Fausto v. Credigy Services Corp. (N.D. Cal. 2009) 598 F.Supp.2d 1049,\nL139@P5: 1053 [reaching same conclusion with respect to similar language in FDCPA].) Indeed,\nL140@P5: the Rosenthal Act, like the FDCPA, was specifically designed “to ‘eliminate the recurring\nL141@P5: problem of debt collectors dunning the wrong person or attempting to collect debts which\nL142@P5: the consumer has already paid.’” (Swanson v. South Oregon Credit Service, Inc.\nL143@P5: (9th Cir.1988) 869 F.2d 1222, 1225; see also Paredes v. Credit Consulting Services, Inc.\nL144@P5: (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 410, 425 (Paredes) [“The Rosenthal Act is modeled on, and\nL145@P5: applies more broadly than, the federal FDCPA.”].) “It is difficult to conceive of a more\nL146@P5: unfair debt collection practice than dunning the wrong person” or dunning a person for\nL147@P5: debt already paid. (Davis v. Midland Funding, LLC (E.D. Cal. 2014) 41 F.Supp.3d 919,\nL148@P5: 925, italics in original.)\nL149@P5: Turning to the second component of consumer debt, namely the reason for\nL150@P5: which the debt is alleged to be owing, the complaint alleges the outstanding monies were\nL151@P5: payments purportedly owed under the solar agreement. Pursuant to the solar agreement,\nL152@P5-6: Kilowatt agreed to install a solar panel system on the home, operate, maintain and repair 7\nL153@P6: it, and sell to the homeowner all electric energy produced by it. In exchange, the\nL154@P6: homeowner agreed to pay for all electricity produced and to use such electricity\nL155@P6: “primarily for personal, family or household purposes[.]” Pre-payment was not required,\nL156@P6: and each monthly payment was for past service (e.g., June bill was for electricity\nL157@P6: generated in May).\nL158@P6: Given this structure, the solar agreement embodies a transaction in which\nL159@P6: services were acquired on credit primarily for personal, family, or household purposes—\nL160@P6: i.e., a consumer credit transaction. (See Davidson, supra, 21 Cal.App.5th at p. 296\nL161@P6: [concluding “on credit” as used in Rosenthal Act means “obtaining something of value\nL162@P6: without immediate payment on the promise to make a payment or payments in the\nL163@P6: future”]; Merriam-Webster Dict. Online (2023) <https://www.merriam\u0002webster.com/dictionary/credit> [as of June 7, 2023] [defining “credit” as “the provision\nL164@P6: of money, goods, or services with the expectation of future payment”].) Once again, it is\nL165@P6: irrelevant all money due under the solar agreement was already paid. And it is equally\nL166@P6: irrelevant that plaintiff was not a party to the solar agreement. The focus is what\nL167@P6: defendant’s debt collection practices said about the supposed debt, namely that it was due\nL168@P6: and owing under the solar agreement.\nL169@P6: Defendant argues sustaining of the demurrer should be affirmed because\nL170@P6: plaintiff did not, and cannot, allege a transaction between him and defendant. But\nL171@P6: nothing in the Rosenthal Act requires a transaction between the debt collector and the\nL172@P6: alleged debtor. Quite the opposite, the legislation encompasses debt collection activity\nL173@P6: engaged in on behalf of another. (§ 1788.2, subd. (c).)\nL174@P6: Because the operative complaint stated a cause of action under the\nL175@P6: Rosenthal Act, defendant’s demurrer should have been overruled as to that cause of\nL176@P6-7: action. The trial court erred in doing otherwise.8\nL177@P7: DISPOSITION\nL178@P7: The judgment is reversed. On remand, the trial court shall vacate the\nL179@P7: judgment and the order sustaining defendant’s demurrer in full and shall enter a new\nL180@P7: order overruling the demurrer as to plaintiff’s Rosenthal Act cause of action. HODGE, Plaintiff, v. TRAVEL + LEISURE CO., Defendant.\nL107: \nL108: [Input]\nL109: \nL110: EDWARD J. DAVILA, United States District Judge\nL111: \nL112: ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO DISMISS WITH LEAVE TO AMEND\nL113: \nL114: RE: ECF NO. 30\nL115: \nL116: EDWARD J. DAVILA, United States District Judge\nL117: Plaintiff Vernicky Hodge filed this lawsuit in response to allegedly harassing calls from Defendant Travel + Leisure Co. According to Hodge, Defendant called multiple times a day to collect on late timeshare payments in violation of both the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) and the Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (RFDCPA). Defendant moved to dismiss Hodge's claims under Rule 12(b)(6).\nL118: The Court finds Defendant's motion suitable for decision without oral argument and therefore VACATES the upcoming motion hearing. See L.R. 7-1(b). Because Hodge has not pled facts showing that the TCPA or RFDCPA apply, the Court GRANTS Defendant's motion WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.\nL119: I. BACKGROUND\nL120: Around 2020, Hodge purchased two timeshare properties through Defendant. Am. Compl. ¶ 10, ECF No. 29. In making those purchases, Hodge agreed to make certain monthly payments tied to timeshare-related services. Id. ¶ 12. Although Hodge often made her payments on time, occasionally she would be a few days late. Id. ¶ 11. Hodge claims that whenever she was late, Defendant would repeatedly call her cell phone-sometimes upwards of three times a day-to collect on her missed payment. Id. ¶¶ 13, 22, 26.\nL121: Allegedly, Defendant used artificial or prerecorded voice messages on those calls. Id. ¶ 17-18.\nL122: II. LEGAL STANDARD\nL123: To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion for failure to state a claim, a complaint must contain sufficient factual allegations to make out a plausible legal claim. cite16†Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (citation omitted). In determining whether the complaint states a plausible claim, courts “accept as true all factual allegations in the complaint and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party.” cite17†Retail Prop. Tr. v. United Bhd.\nL124: of Carpenters & Joiners of Am., 768 F.3d 938, 945 (9th Cir. 2014). But courts “are not bound to accept as true a legal conclusion couched as a factual allegation.” cite16†Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citation omitted).\nL125: III. DISCUSSION\nL126: \nL127: A. TCPA\nL128: As relevant to Hodge's claims, calls can violate the TCPA in two ways: (a) by using an automatic telephone dialing system (ATDS) or (b) by using an artificial or prerecorded voice. cite18†Trim v. Reward Zone USA LLC, 76 F.4th 1157, 1160 (9th Cir. 2023). Hodge does not claim that Defendant used an ATDS, so only the prohibition on artificial or prerecorded voices is at issue here. However, the factual allegations supporting Hodge's assertion that Defendant used artificial or prerecorded voices are sparse.\nL129: Hodge makes only two factual allegations. First, Hodge alleges that “she would be left prerecorded messages purportedly from ‘Sarah from Wyndham Vacation Resorts'” when declining to answer Defendant's calls. Am. Compl. ¶ 17. Second, Hodge alleges that “[o]n answered calls, Plaintiff would similarly be greeted by an artificial or prerecorded voice message.” Id. ¶ 18.\nL130: Otherwise, Hodge merely asserts in conclusory fashion that “Defendant used prerecorded and/or artificial voice messages” and “plac[ed] dozens of phone calls to Plaintiff's cellular phone using prerecorded messages.” Id. ¶¶ 32-33. These allegations are little more than “labels and conclusions” insufficient to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6). cite19†Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007).\nL131: That is because Hodge merely recites one of the TCPA's requirements- use of an artificial or prerecorded voice-without providing factual detail from which that conclusion can be inferred. Id. (“formulaic recitation” of a claim's elements is not enough).\nL132: Hodge suggests she need not plead more than the bare assertion that Defendant used an artificial or prerecorded voice message, citing to cases that apply an “easier” standard where “merely . . . alleging that a defendant used an artificial or prerecorded voice suffices at the pleading stage.” Opp'n 4, ECF No. 31 (quoting Slominski v. Globe Life Inc., No. 7:23-cv-1081, 2024 WL 556978, at *6 (E.D. N.C. Feb. 12, 2024)). The Court respectfully disagrees with those cases.\nL133: It would be inconsistent with Twombly to allow such conclusory allegations to satisfy a plaintiff's pleading burden. And district courts within this Circuit largely agree. E.g., Andersen v. Nexa Mortg., LLC, No. 8:24-cv-00619, 2024 WL 3762098, at *4 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 12, 2024); cite20†Rogers v. Assurance IQ, LLC, No. 2:21-cv-00823 , cite21†2023 WL 2646468, at *3-4 (W.D. Wash. Mar. 27, 2023); cite22†Allison v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., No. 22-cv-0510 , cite23†2022 WL 10756885, at *3 (S.D. Cal. Oct. 18, 2022).\nL134: Moreover, applying this higher standard does not create an impossible burden-there are several cases identifying the types of facts that can satisfy the higher standard while still being within the plaintiff's knowledge even at the pleading stage. E.g., Smith v. Am.-Amicable Life Ins. Co. of Tex., No. 22-cv-333, 2022 WL 1003762, at *2 (E.D. Pa. Apr. 4, 2022); Johansen v. Vivant, Inc., No. 12-cv-7159, 2012 WL 6590551, at *3 (N.D. Ill.Dec. 18, 2012).\nL135: Accordingly, the Court DISMISSES Hodge's TCPA claim.\nL136: \nL137: Hodge tries to argue that she included factual allegations, such as that the messages she received “contained identical language” and were “duplicative.” Opp'n 5. However, those facts are not alleged in Hodge's complaint. They are raised only in her opposition, and “[statements made in an opposition brief cannot amend the complaint.” cite24†Rodriguez v. In turn, the RFDCPA defines “consumer credit transaction” as “a transaction between a natural person and another person in which property, services, or money is acquired on credit by that natural person from the other person primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.” Cal. Civ. Code § 1788.2(e).\nL141: The key language from that definition is the phrase, “on credit,” which the RFDCPA does not further define. In interpreting this language, the California state courts agree that “on credit” should be defined broadly, but they formulate its definition in different ways. For example, one court defined “on credit” to mean “obtaining something of value without immediate payment on the promise to make a payment or payments in the future.” cite28†Davidson v. Seterus, Inc., 21 Cal.App. 5th 283, 296 (2018).\nL142: Another held that acquiring something “on credit” means “acquir[ing] something without paying for it.” cite29†Gouskos v. Aptos Vill. Garage, Inc., 94 Cal.App.4th 754, 759 (2001). The remainder of California courts to address the RFDCPA's language generally apply some combination of these two definitions. cite30†See Hagey v. Solar Serv. Experts, LLC, 94 Cal.App. 5th 1303, 1309 (2023) (Davidson definition); cite31†Paredes v. Civ. Code § 1788.2(1) . Only “consumer debt” is relevant here, and it is defined as “money, property, or their equivalent, due . . . or alleged to be due . . . from a natural person by reason of a consumer credit transaction.” Id. § 1788.2(f) (emphasis added). Similarly, cite27†§ 1788.17 applies to “consumer debt” and therefore to “consumer credit transactions.”\nL145: Regardless of the exact formulation used, these precedents make clear that a “consumer credit transaction” includes at least two fundamental elements: (1) acquisition of something of value without immediate payment, and (2) an agreement between the parties to the transaction that the consumer will pay in the future.\nL146: Applying this understanding, the Court finds that Hodge has failed to allege that her late timeshare payments qualify as “consumer credit transactions.” In her complaint, Hodge describes her monthly timeshare payments as going towards “services associated with the timeshares [she] purchased.” Am.
