 2    II.   LEGAL STANDARD
 3          A.     Motion to Dismiss
 4          Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) permits a party to raise by motion the
 5    defense that the complaint “fail[s] to state a claim upon which relief can be granted,”
 6    generally referred to as a motion to dismiss. The Court evaluates whether a complaint
 7    states a cognizable legal theory and sufficient facts in light of Federal Rule of Civil
 8    Procedure 8(a), which requires a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that the
 9    pleader is entitled to relief.” Although Rule 8 “does not require ‘detailed factual
10    allegations,’” it does require “more than an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-
11    harmed-me accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl.
12    Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)). A “formulaic recitation of the elements of
13    a cause of action” is insufficient. Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555). “Nor does a
14    complaint suffice if it tenders ‘naked assertion[s]’ devoid of ‘further factual
15    enhancement.’” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 557).
16          In reviewing the plausibility of a complaint, courts “accept factual allegations in
17    the complaint as true and construe the pleadings in the light most favorable to the
18    nonmoving party.” Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co., 519 F.3d 1025, 1031
19    (9th Cir. 2008). Nonetheless, courts do not “accept as true allegations that are merely
20    conclusory, unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable inferences.” In re Gilead
21    Scis. Secs. Litig., 536 F.3d 1049, 1055 (9th Cir. 2008) (quoting Sprewell v. Golden State
22    Warriors, 266 F.3d 979, 988 (9th Cir. 2001)). The Court also need not accept as true
23    allegations that contradict matter properly subject to judicial notice or allegations
24    contradicting the exhibits attached to the complaint. Sprewell, 266 F.3d at 988.
25          “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual
26    matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Id.
27    (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). A claim is facially plausible when the facts pleaded
28    “allow[] the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the

 1    misconduct alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556). That is
 2    not to say that the claim must be probable, but there must be “more than a sheer
 3    possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully.” Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).
 4           When a motion to dismiss is granted, “leave to amend should be granted ‘unless
 5    the court determines that the allegation of other facts consistent with the challenged
 6    pleading could not possibly cure the deficiency.’” DeSoto v. Yellow Freight Sys., Inc.,
 7    957 F.2d 655, 658 (9th Cir. 1992) (quoting Schreiber Distrib. Co. v. Serv-Well Furniture
 8    Co., 806 F.2d 1393, 1401 (9th Cir. 1986)). The Court may deny leave to amend where an
 9    amendment would be futile. Desoto, 957 F.2d at 658 (citation omitted).
10           B.    Motion to Strike
11           Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12 provides that a court “may strike from a
12    pleading an insufficient defense or any redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous
13    matter.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(f). “’Redundant’ allegations are those that are needlessly
14    repetitive or wholly foreign to the issues involved in the action.” California Dep’t of
15    Toxic Substances Control v. Alco Pac., Inc., 217 F. Supp. 2d 1028, 1033 (C.D. Cal. 2002)
16    (citation omitted). An “immaterial” matter “has no essential or important relationship to
17    the claim for relief or the defenses being pleaded.” Fantasy, Inc. v. Fogerty, 984 F.2d
18    1524, 1527 (9th Cir. 1993), rev’d on other grounds by Fogerty v. Fantasy, Inc.,
19    510 U.S. 517 (1994) (citation omitted). “Impertinent” statements “do not pertain, and are
20    not necessary, to the issues in question.” Id. (citation omitted). “Scandalous” matter casts
21    “a cruelly derogatory light on a party or other person.” In re 2TheMart.com, Inc. Sec.
22    Litig., 114 F. Supp. 2d 955, 965 (C.D. Cal. 2000). “[M]otions to strike are generally not
23    granted unless it is clear that the matter sought to be stricken could have no possible
24    bearing on the subject matter of the litigation.” Gaines v. AT&T Mobility Servs., LLC,
25    424 F. Supp. 3d 1004, 1014 (S.D. Cal. 2019) (citation omitted).

 4          A.     Necessary and Indispensable Parties
 5          Defendants contend that the SPEs are necessary and indispensable parties because
 6    Plaintiffs’ request for relief is not possible in their absence. ECF No. 52-1 at 7. Plaintiffs
 7    oppose, arguing that joinder is not necessary, that they do not seek to hold the SPEs liable
 8    and do not seek recovery against the SPEs, and that the Court previously considered and
 9    rejected an identical argument by Defendants when they sought to dismiss the First
10    Amended Complaint. ECF No. 53 at 14, 18.
11          Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 19 provides that a party is necessary if:
12          (A) in that person’s absence, the court cannot accord complete relief among
            existing parties; or
13
            (B) that person claims an interest relating to the subject of the action and is
14          so situated that disposing of the action in the person’s absence may:
                   (i) as a practical matter impair or impede the person’s ability to
15
            protect the interest; or
16                 (ii) leave an existing party subject to a substantial risk of incurring
            double, multiple, or otherwise inconsistent obligations because of the
17
            interest.
18    Fed. R. Civ. P. 19(a)(1). Failure to join a necessary party under Rule 19 is grounds for a
19    motion to dismiss. Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(7).
20          If the party is necessary and cannot be joined, “the court must determine whether,
21    in equity and good conscience, the action should proceed among the parties or should be
22    dismissed” because the party is indispensable. Fed. R. Civ. P. 19(b). To do so, the court
23    must consider the following factors:
24          (1) the extent to which a judgment rendered in the person’s absence might
            prejudice that person or the existing parties;
25
            (2) the extent to which any prejudice could be lessened or avoided by:
26                 (A) protective provisions in the judgment;
                   (B) shaping the relief; or
27
                   (C) other measures;

 1          (3) whether a judgment rendered in the person's absence would be adequate;
            and

 3          dismissed for nonjoinder.
 4    Id. “The inquiry is a practical one and fact specific, and is designed to avoid the harsh
 5    results of rigid application. The moving party has the burden of persuasion in arguing for
 6    dismissal.” Clinton v. Babbitt, 180 F.3d 1081, 1088 (9th Cir. 1999) (citation omitted).
 7          Defendants argue that joinder of the SPEs is required because of the form of relief
 8    Plaintiff seeks, but the Court does not agree. Defendants contend that the SPEs are
 9    necessary because Plaintiffs seek the return of the entirety of all Plaintiffs’ and Plaintiff
10    Class’s security deposits, which they claim are owned by the SPEs.3 ECF No. 52-1 at 16–
11    17; ECF No. 49 at 80. According to Defendants, this ownership means that the SPEs have
12    “an interest relating to the subject of the action” and that the “court cannot accord
13    complete relief” pursuant to Rule 19. ECF No. 52-1 at 16–17. The Court is not
14    persuaded. First, security deposits are owned by tenants—not the SPEs—and held by
15    landlords. Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5(d); In re Choy, 569 B.R. 169, 183–84 (Bankr. N.D.
16    Cal. 2017) (“California Civil Code § 1950.5(d) requires that a residential landlord ‘hold’
17    a tenant’s security deposit.”); Small Prop. Owners of San Francisco v. City & Cnty. of
18    San Francisco, 141 Cal. App. 4th 1388, 1398 n.4 (2006) (noting that landlords do not
19    own the security deposits). Second, the SPEs have not claimed an interest in this action.

26    F. Supp. 2d 978, 990 (N.D. Cal. 2009). “[W]hile there is little authority on this issue
27    within the Ninth Circuit, decisions from courts in other jurisdictions have made clear that
