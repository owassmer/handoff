# Independent review of historical federal incorporation and COVID debt transfers

Reviewed October 1, 2026. **Pass after the recipient-specific correction.** The two substantive legal conclusions are defensible and operationally determinate: (1) a covered, nonexempt collector's first debt communication served on the consumer as a pleading triggers California's incorporated historical validation duty; (2) Civil Code section 1788.66 survives repeal of the separate CCP procedural chapter and continues to prohibit transfers of its qualifying historical debt. Neither conclusion is deferred for want of a fact-identical appellate case.

This is a separately tasked same-model-family review, not independent human or separate-model-family validation. I read the authors' conclusions, actual saved statutory/opinion passages, and independently searched contrary authority. I communicated the material recipient omission and re-read the resulting author correction. I did not edit the author memoranda. Added review-prefixed source captures and this report are reviewer artifacts.

## 1. Pleading-only validation: accepted with an actual recipient branch

The decisive enactment is AB 1669, Stats. 2000, chapter 688, section 1, printed page 4532. It expressly freezes the federal references at January 1, 2001. The current official Civil Code section 1788.17 still contains that date and the precise section 1692a(6)(A)/(B) person/principal exceptions for g and e(11). Historical section 1692g(a) contains a consumer-communication trigger, five-day sending requirement, five listed notice elements, and the included-information/paid-debt exceptions. Historical e(11), in contrast, expressly excludes formal pleadings. I checked these actual saved texts. [Current official section 1788.17](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1788.17.)

The strongest contrary argument is substantive, not merely that Congress amended the statute later: *Vega v. McKay*, 351 F.3d 1334, 1337–1338 (11th Cir. 2003), interprets the old text to exclude litigation, while *Velazquez* suggests the 2006 amendment may clarify rather than change its meaning. I read Vega's full saved opinion and Velazquez's actual pages 6–7. An express temporal freeze does not, by itself, prove which of two readings of the old wording is correct. But the broad historical communication definition, the neighboring express e(11) exception, and *Heintz*'s rejection of a general litigation exclusion favor the author's reading. The en banc *Thomas* majority applies those specific considerations to actual service and explains workable notice delivery. Velazquez declined the state claim without prejudice after dismissing the federal claim; its skeptical discussion is not a merits holding that California exempts initial pleadings. The 2006 g(d) exemption is operative current federal text, not an amendment of California's frozen incorporation.

The March 31, 2000 FTC **Commission** advisory opinion supplies unusually relevant pre-freeze interpretation. I read the saved OCR against the opinion's identified second-issue discussion, PDF pages 12–13, printed pages 3–4. It directly rejects reliance on the earlier staff commentary after Heintz and says a debt-bearing court document served on a consumer can be the first communication requiring notice. That is persuasive evidence of the old text's interpretation, not binding California precedent, a federal legislative enactment, or an automatically incorporated regulation. This temporal and institutional distinction materially weakens Vega's reliance on the 1988 staff position. The conclusion remains independently supported without elevating the FTC opinion into binding authority.

I also checked the actual 2006 Public Law 109-351 section 802 additions and historical g(b). The old thirty-day period is not a universal waiting period before suit. A qualifying written dispute or original-creditor request produces the specified collection suspension; otherwise collection can continue subject to nonovershadowing and other law. A notice must not imply that its dispute window replaces a judicial response deadline. Thomas's historical sample discusses then-current court deadlines; it is reasoning, not a source for today's number of response days. Federal section 1692n permits greater state protection; accurate compliance with a state notice duty is not prohibited by the current federal pleading exemption.

### Material defect raised and corrected

The original memo's generic warning about representation did not resolve the recipient distinction. I independently retrieved *Guerrero v. RJM Acquisitions*, 499 F.3d 926, 935–939 (9th Cir. 2007), and the official *Donohue v. Quick Collect*, 592 F.3d 1027, 1031–1032 (9th Cir. 2010). Donohue expressly distinguishes counsel-only communications from a complaint personally served on the debtor. Guerrero permits the particular counsel-only responsive communication despite a pending verification dispute. [Official Donohue opinion, printed pages 1006–1008](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/01/13/09-35183.pdf); [Guerrero judicial text](https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/05-15121/920070822).

The author's new recipient-specific section resolves this correctly:

- A first communication solely to retained counsel does not itself trigger the historical g(a) consumer-notice clock. This is a reasoned application of the statutory recipient language and Guerrero, not a claim Guerrero decided that exact g(a) issue.
- Actual consumer-directed complaint service remains a communication even when the consumer has counsel. Filing an unseen paper with the court alone is not the same factual event. A previous trigger does not disappear when counsel appears.
- Responsive negotiation solely through counsel is not automatically barred by g(b). Guerrero does not authorize every coercive litigation step, default application, garnishment, third-party contact, or threatened return to direct debtor contact.
- The independently applicable representation restriction still controls the permissible delivery route. Historical c(a)(2) identifies actual consent/permission and attorney-response conditions. Thomas, printed page 10, discusses court permission for required personal service; it does not establish universal permission to send unrelated collection correspondence directly to represented consumers. Trigger analysis and lawful delivery must remain separate.

I accept these branches. Actual recipient, service mode, first-contact history and attorney status are factual predicates to acquire, not unresolved legal interpretations. Civil service completion rules must not be silently substituted for proof of when and how the debt information was communicated. No finding here mandates unnecessary direct contact or a blanket pause of counsel-led settlement.

### Additional California litigation-privilege challenge: rejected

The state-law nature of the duty does not create an escape through Civil Code section 47(b). Published *People v. Persolve*, 218 Cal.App.4th 1267 (2013), opinion pages 6–9, applies *Komarova*, 175 Cal.App.4th 324, 337–340, and holds the privilege does not shelter the specifically prohibited debt-collection conduct underlying the claims there. Velazquez itself recognizes Komarova in page 7 footnote 2. This is affirmative contrary-authority resolution, not reliance solely on federal preemption. It does not eliminate the privilege for every derivative tort or establish liability absent a substantive statutory violation. [Persolve primary judicial opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2013/f064571.html).

## 2. COVID assignment survival: accepted

The proposed dynamic-incorporation objection is real: *People v. Rojas*, 15 Cal.5th 561 (2023), applying *In re Jovan B.*, makes legislative intent controlling rather than treating a code citation as automatically static. I read the saved Rojas opinion's discussion of specific/general incorporation and its distinction from *Palermo*, 32 Cal.2d 53, 58–60. The memorandum correctly engages that objection.

Here the AB 832 enactment supplies stronger contrary evidence to expiration than a code-number presumption. Sections 2 and 19 expressly sunset the broad Civil Code 1788.65 restriction and the CCP chapter, respectively. Section 3 retains the narrower 1788.66 restriction without a sunset. The Legislative Counsel's Digest paragraph (2) says only the general transfer restriction will be repealed. The surviving provision defines a closed historical debt period and historical income/assistance facts. Deleting the incorporated definition after 2025 would silently nullify that deliberately retained class-specific restriction. The static historical definition is the better reading under Rojas's intent analysis and Palermo's repeal rule, not an exception to them. [Current official section 1788.66](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1788.66.)

I checked AB 832 sections 2, 3, 14 and 19 directly. CCP 1179.02(c) reaches unpaid rent **and other unpaid financial obligations under the tenancy**, based on when due; it does not require the obligation itself to be labeled pandemic relief. The incorporated tenant definition has commercial/transient occupancy exclusions. A damage or utility item cannot be included merely because it shares an account with covered rent; its obligation source and due date must independently qualify. Nor does the incorporation preserve every expired eviction procedure. An historical defense based on conduct occurring while a procedure was in force is a separate temporal question, not automatically erased by repeal and not automatically continued by retaining a definition.

### Historical ERA predicates: affirmative interpretation

I independently read saved 2021 15 USC 9058a(k)(3), 9058c(f)(2), and the incorporated 42 USC 1437a(b)(2)(A) income definition. Section 1788.66 uses ERA1 **or** ERA2 historical eligibility, plus its own household-income condition for 2020 **or** 2021. These are separate logical gates:

- ERA1 requires the residential rental obligation; a qualifying unemployment-benefit or COVID-linked financial-hardship condition in a household member; housing-instability/homelessness risk; and the statutory income condition. Its income-determination subsection recognizes 2020 income or qualifying monthly income with redetermination. Do not replace it with only a current-income test.
- ERA2 has its own eligibility formulation, including hardship **during or due to** the pandemic and the referenced low-income-family definition. That definition includes household-size adjustments and authorized local income-ceiling variations; it is not simply a copied ERA1 definition. The state's independent 80%-AMI 2020/2021 gate must still be satisfied.
- The counterfactual historical language does not demand actual grant receipt, an open program today, or an actually filed historical application. Conversely, low income alone does not prove the unemployment/hardship and housing-instability predicates. Ordinary evidence supporting those historical facts is necessary.
- Program funding exhaustion or a discretionary prioritization decision does not rewrite substantive eligibility. Actual prior assistance requires ledger/duplication examination: paid amounts are no longer unpaid, while remaining distinct unpaid obligations are not automatically transferable merely because the household previously received some assistance.

These conclusions apply the named statutes to a historical counterfactual; they do not promise a new grant or treat every old balance as prohibited. Missing household records lead to factual acquisition, not a suspended legal conclusion about whether 1788.66 survived.

### Contrary current official material examined without misattribution

An independent search located the Judicial Council's October 2025 form-revision report. The web tool returned 410, but direct retrieval recovered the full PDF, now saved as `review-JCC-2025.pdf`. The statement that section 1788.66 does not expire appears in **Bay Area Legal Aid's public comment**, printed page 61, and even mislabels the code as CCP. It is not a judicial holding or the committee's own adoption of that interpretation.

The committee's pages 7–8 instead explain form revocation using ordinary four-year limitations assumptions and acknowledge that general forms remain usable for any surviving actions. That is not repeal of 1788.66 and does not turn every old obligation into an extinguished debt. A particular limitations result depends on its own accrual, tolling and other facts. The survival conclusion here rests on the actual enactment and governing interpretation principles, not the comment or the continued availability of a form. [Official Judicial Council report](https://jcc.legistar.com/View.ashx?GUID=832C0E1B-56C5-4980-AEB4-A5A1F113E5B8&ID=14853481&M=F).
