L46: cite11†Justia Opinion Summary†connect.justia.com L47: At issue in this appeal was whether a mortgage servicer could be considered a "debt collector" under California's Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (the Rosenthal Act; Civ. Code,1 sec. 1788 et seq.).
L48: There was a split of authority among the many federal district courts that have considered the issue, and there was “a paucity of California authority addressing the question.” In this case, plaintiff Edward Davidson brought a putative class action against Seterus and its parent company, International Business Machines, Inc. (IBM), alleging that the defendants violated the Act and the Unfair Competition Law (UCL).
L49: The defendants demurred to Davidson's complaint, arguing that neither of them was a " 'debt collector' " who engages in " 'debt collection' " under the Act. The trial court sustained the defendants' demurrer, concluding that the defendants "are not 'debt collectors' because servicing a mortgage is not a form of collecting 'consumer debts.' " On appeal, Davidson contended the trial court erred in determining that mortgage servicers were not "debt collectors" under the Rosenthal Act.
L50: The Court of Appeal ultimately agreed with Davidson's contention, in no small part due to the Court’s adherence to "the general rule that civil statutes for the protection of the public are, generally, broadly construed in favor of that protective purpose." The Court therefore reversed the trial court and remanded this case for further proceedings.

L61: INTRODUCTION At issue in this appeal is whether mortgage servicers can be &quot;debt collectors&quot; under California&#039;s Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (the Rosenthal Act; Civ. Code,1 § 1788 et seq.). There is a split of authority among the many federal district courts that have considered the issue, and there is a paucity of California authority addressing the question.
L62: In this case, the plaintiff, Edward Davidson, brought a putative class action against Seterus and its parent company, International Business Machines, Inc. (IBM), alleging that the defendants violated the Act and the Unfair Competition Law (UCL).
L63: The defendants demurred to Davidson&#039;s complaint, arguing that neither of them is a &quot; &#039;debt collector&#039; &quot; who engages in &quot; &#039;debt collection&#039; &quot; under the Act.2 The trial court sustained the defendants&#039; demurrer, concluding that the defendants &quot;are not &#039;debt collectors&#039; because servicing a mortgage is not a form of collecting &#039;consumer debts.&#039; &quot;3 On appeal, Davidson contends that the trial court erred in determining that mortgage servicers are not &quot;debt collectors&quot; under the Rosenthal Act.
L64: We ultimately agree with Davidson&#039;s contention, in no small part due to our adherence to &quot;the general 1 Further statutory references are to the Civil Code unless otherwise indicated. 2 IBM also demurred on the ground that the facts as alleged are insufficient to state a cause of action as to it because the complaint does not establish that IBM was a debt collector or that it may be held liable for Seterus&#039;s conduct.
L65: 3 The trial court also concluded that the claims against IBM failed, independently, because Davidson failed to plead sufficient facts to hold parent company IBM liable for the actions of its subsidiary, Seterus. 2 rule that civil statutes for the protection of the public are, generally, broadly construed in favor of that protective purpose.&quot; (People ex rel. Lungren v. Superior Court (1996) 14 Cal.4th 294, 313, italics added; see Florez v. Linens &#039;N Things, Inc.
L66: (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 447, 450.) It is clear that the Rosenthal Act is a civil statute that was enacted for the protection of the public, and in interpreting it, we are mindful of the fact that, to the extent that the statutory language is ambiguous, the statute should be construed broadly in favor of protecting the public.
L67: Given this principle, and the fact that the Rosenthal Act&#039;s definitional language is sufficiently broad to include mortgage lenders and/or mortgage servicers within its purview, we conclude that mortgage lenders and mortgage servicers can be &quot;debt collectors&quot; under the Rosenthal Act. We therefore reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for further proceedings. II. FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND A.

L81: Procedural background 1. The complaint Davidson filed a putative class action on behalf of California residents who have been subjected to the defendants&#039; allegedly unlawful debt collection practices, in September 2016. Davidson&#039;s complaint set forth causes of action for violations of the Rosenthal Act and the UCL (Bus. &amp; Prof. Code, § 17200 et seq.).
L82: Davidson alleges that the defendants are &quot; &#039;debt collectors&#039; &quot; who are subject to the requirements of the Rosenthal Act, and that they were acting as &quot; &#039;debt collectors&#039; &quot; when they undertook the allegedly unlawful conduct described in his complaint.
L83: Davidson alleges that the defendants&#039; conduct violates the Rosenthal Act&#039;s prohibitions against &quot; &#039;[c]ausing a telephone to ring repeatedly or continuously to annoy the person called&#039; &quot; and &quot; &#039;communicating by telephone . . . with the debtor with such frequency as to be unreasonable and to constitute an harassment to the debtor under the circumstances.&#039; &quot; 7 (§ 1788.11, subds.
L84: (d), (e).) He further alleges that the defendants violated the prohibition against &quot; &#039;[t]he false representation that information concerning a debtor&#039;s failure or alleged failure to pay a consumer debt has been or is about to be referred to a consumer reporting agency&#039; &quot; (§ 1788.13, subd. (f)), and that defendants violated the Rosenthal Act&#039;s requirement that they comply with the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (the FDCPA; 15 U.S.C. § 1692 et seq.).
L85: Davidson&#039;s UCL cause of action is based on the alleged violations of the Rosenthal Act which, he asserts, constitute per se violations of the UCL. 2. The defendants&#039; demurrer The defendants filed a demurrer to Davidson&#039;s complaint on May 11, 2016.
L86: The defendants argued that Davidson is unable to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action under the Rosenthal Act because neither Seterus nor IBM is a &quot; &#039;debt collector&#039; &quot; who &quot; &#039;engages in debt collection&#039; &quot; under the Rosenthal Act. IBM also argued that the cause of action against IBM is too uncertain because the complaint does not allege sufficient facts to establish that IBM is a debt collector and that it is liable for Seterus&#039;s conduct.
L87: After full briefing, the trial court issued a tentative ruling on the defendants&#039; demurrer on September 6, 2016, in which it indicated its intention to sustain the demurrer without leave to amend. The court held a hearing on the matter on September 9, and subsequently adopted its tentative ruling.
L88: In sustaining the demurrer without leave to amend, the trial court acknowledged the existence of a split of authority in the federal courts as to whether the Rosenthal Act&#039;s definition of &quot;debt collector&quot; includes a 8 mortgage servicer. However, the court agreed with those courts that have concluded that a mortgage servicer may not be considered to be a &quot;debt collector&quot; under the Rosenthal Act.
L89: The court determined that because Davidson&#039;s UCL claim was derivative of his Rosenthal Act claim, the complaint failed to state a cause of action. The court also ruled, in the alternative, that as to IBM, the complaint failed to plead sufficient facts to demonstrate that IBM could be held liable for the actions of its subsidiary, Seterus.
L90: The trial court further determined that no amendment could cure the deficiencies of the complaint, and that the demurrer should therefore be sustained without leave to amend. The trial court entered judgment in favor of the defendants on October 5, 2016. Davidson filed a timely notice of appeal. III. DISCUSSION A.
L91: Legal standards on appeal from the sustaining of a demurrer without leave to amend We apply the following well-established law in reviewing a trial court&#039;s order sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend: &quot;We independently review the ruling on a demurrer and determine de novo whether the complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a cause of action.
L92: [Citation.] We assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations, facts that reasonably can be inferred from those expressly pleaded, and matters of which judicial notice has been taken. [Citation.] We construe the pleading in a reasonable manner and read the allegations in context.&quot; (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp.
L93: (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 111 (Fremont Indemnity).) 9 Where the complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action and the trial court has sustained a demurrer without leave to amend, &quot;we decide whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment: if it can be, the trial court has abused its discretion and we reverse; if not, there has been no abuse of discretion and we affirm.
L94: [Citations.] The burden of proving such reasonable possibility is squarely on the plaintiff.&quot; (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) B. The trial court erred in concluding that a mortgage servicer can never be a &quot;debt collector&quot; under the Rosenthal Act 1. Statutory interpretation standards This case hinges on the proper interpretation of the Rosenthal Act. &quot;The basic rules of statutory construction are well established.
L95: &#039;When construing a statute, a court seeks to determine and give effect to the intent of the enacting legislative body.&#039; [Citation.] &#039; &quot;We first examine the words themselves because the statutory language is generally the most reliable indicator of legislative intent.
L96: [Citation.] The words of the statute should be given their ordinary and usual meaning and should be construed in their statutory context.&quot; [Citation.] If the plain, commonsense meaning of a statute&#039;s words is unambiguous, the plain meaning controls.&#039; &quot; (People v.
L97: King (2006) 38 Cal.4th 617, 622.) &quot;If, however, &#039;the statutory language may reasonably be given more than one interpretation, &quot; &#039; &quot;courts may consider various extrinsic aids, including the purpose of the statute, the evils to be remedied, the legislative history, public policy, and the statutory 10 scheme encompassing the statute.&quot; &#039; &quot; &#039; &quot; (People v. Cornett (2012) 53 Cal.4th 1261, 1265.) 2.
L98: Statutory and legal framework The Rosenthal Act was enacted &quot;to prohibit debt collectors from engaging in unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the collection of consumer debts.&quot; (§ 1788.1, subd. (b).) The Rosenthal Act is &quot; &#039;a remedial statute [that] should be interpreted broadly in order to effectuate its purpose.&#039; &quot; (Komarova v. National Credit Acceptance, Inc.
L99: (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 324, 340 (Komarova), italics added.) It was enacted in 1977, the same year that its federal counterpart, the FDCPA, was enacted. (In re Landry (Bankr. E.D.Cal. 2013) 493 B.R. 541, 570 (Landry).)7 In addition to its other requirements and prohibitions, the Rosenthal Act generally requires debt collectors to comply with the provisions of the FDCPA.
L100: (§ 1788.17.) The Rosenthal Act defines a &quot;debt collector&quot; as &quot;any person who, in the ordinary course of business, regularly, on behalf of himself or herself or others, engaged in debt collection.&quot; (§ 1788.2, subd. (c), italics added.) The Rosenthal Act defines the term &quot;debt collection&quot; as follows: &quot;any act or practice in connection with the collection of consumer debt.&quot; (§ 1788.2, subd.
L101: (b).) 7 The purpose of the FDCPA is &quot;to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors, to insure [sic] that those debt collectors who refrain from using abusive debt collection practices are not competitively disadvantaged, and to promote consistent State action to protect consumers against debt collection abuses.&quot; (15 U.S.C. § 1692, subd.
L102: (e).) &quot;A basic tenet of the Act is that all consumers, even those who have mismanaged their financial affairs resulting in default on their debt, deserve &#039;the right to be treated in a reasonable and civil manner.&#039; &quot; (Bass v. Stolper, Koritzinsky, Brewster &amp; Neider, S.C.
L103: (7th Cir.1997) 111 F.3d 1322, 1324.) 11 The Rosenthal Act defines &quot;consumer debt&quot; and &quot;consumer credit&quot; as &quot;money, property or their equivalent, due or owing or alleged to be due or owing from a natural person by reason of a consumer credit transaction.&quot; (§ 1788.2, subdivision (f).) The Rosenthal Act further defines the phrase &quot;consumer credit transaction&quot; as &quot;a transaction between a natural person and another person in which property, services, or money is acquired on credit by that natural person from such other person primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.&quot; (§ 1788.2, subd.
L104: (e).) Thus, a debt collector is a person who regularly engages in the act or practice of collecting money, property or their equivalent that is due or owing by a natural person as a result of a transaction between that person and another person, in which the natural person acquired property, services, or money on credit, primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. 3.
L105: Analysis The Rosenthal Act is silent with respect to whether it applies to persons or entities attempting to collect mortgage debt; the definitions of &quot;debt collector,&quot; &quot;consumer debt,&quot; and &quot;consumer credit transaction&quot; make no reference to mortgage lenders and/or servicers or to mortgage debt.
L106: Given that mortgage debt is neither expressly included nor excluded from the definition of &quot;consumer debt,&quot; and that a mortgage transaction is neither expressly included nor excluded from the definition of &quot;consumer credit transaction,&quot; we look to the words of the statute to determine whether they can be understood to either include or exclude from the statute&#039;s purview mortgage debt or mortgage lenders and/or 12 servicers, keeping in mind that the Rosenthal Act &quot; &#039;should be interpreted broadly in order to effectuate its purpose.&#039; &quot; (Komarova, supra, 175 Cal.App.4th at p.
L107: 340.) The phrase, &quot;consumer credit transaction&quot; includes any &quot;transaction between a natural person and another person in which property, services or money is acquired on credit by that natural person from such other person primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.&quot; (§ 1788.2, subd.
L108: (e), italics added.) The Rosenthal Act does not define the phrase &quot;on credit&quot; or the word &quot;credit.&quot; The Oxford English Dictionary defines the phrase &quot;on credit&quot; as &quot;[w]ith an arrangement to pay later.&quot; (Oxford English Dict. (2018) &lt;https://en.oxforddictionaries.com/definition/on_credit&gt; [as of Mar.
L109: 12, 2018].) It also defines &quot;credit&quot; as &quot;[t]he ability of a customer to obtain goods or services before payment, based on the trust that payment will be made in the future.&quot; (Oxford English Dict. (2018) &lt;https://en.oxforddictionaries.com/definition/credit&gt; [as of Mar. 12, 2018].) The American Heritage Dictionary defines &quot;credit&quot; as &quot;a. An arrangement for deferred payment of a loan or purchase . . . . [¶] b. The terms governing such an arrangement . . . . [¶] c.
L110: The time allowed for deferred payment.&quot; (The American Heritage Dict. of the English Language (2018) &lt;https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=credit&gt; [as of Mar. 12, 2018].)8 Based on these definitions, we conclude that the &quot;ordinary and usual meaning&quot; of the phrase &quot;on credit&quot; can be stated as obtaining something of value without immediate payment on the promise to make a payment or payments in the future.
L111: We may thus 8 Courts often consult dictionaries in an effort to determine the usual and ordinary meaning of a word. (Coburn v.
L112: Sievert (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1483, 1499.) 13 construe section 1788.2, subdivision (e) as providing that a &quot;consumer credit transaction&quot; is &quot;a transaction between a natural person and another person in which property, services or money is acquired [without immediate payment and with the promise to pay in the future] by that natural person from such other person primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.&quot; The obtaining of a mortgage involves a transaction in which a natural person acquires property by way of borrowing the funds used to obtain the property from another person or entity without full immediate payment and with the promise to pay back those funds.
L113: It is readily apparent that persons who obtain mortgages very often do so in order to purchase a primary or even secondary residence, which may be considered to be a &quot;personal, family, or household purpose[ ].&quot; (§ 1788.2, subd.
L114: (e).) Further, there is nothing in the statutory definition of &quot;consumer debt&quot; or &quot;consumer credit transaction&quot; that would exclude a debt that otherwise comes within the terms of the Rosenthal Act merely because the debt is secured by real or personal property. At least one other California appellate court has permitted a Rosenthal Act claim to go forward against a lender who was attempting to collect on a residential mortgage loan secured by a junior lien. (See Alborzian v.
L115: JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 29 (Alborzian).)9 9 Although the Alborzian court did not directly address the issue whether a mortgage lender comes within the Rosenthal&#039;s Act&#039;s definition of a &quot;debt collector,&quot; the court clearly treated mortgage debt as falling within the framework of the Rosenthal Act&#039;s protections. (See Alborzian, supra, 235 Cal.App.4th at pp.
L116: 36–38.) 14 Interpreting the Rosenthal Act in this way furthers the purpose of the legislation, which is to prohibit entities who are attempting to collect on a debt from &quot;engaging in unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the collection of [those] debts.&quot; (§ 1788.1, subd.
L117: (b).) Although Seterus&#039;s disclaimer in its correspondence with Davidson that it is a &quot; &#039;debt collector&#039; &quot; that is &quot; &#039;attempting to collect a debt&#039; &quot; (capitalization omitted) is not itself dispositive of the issue, the fact that the defendants indicate to consumers that the activities that they are undertaking with respect to mortgage servicing involves debt collection is in line with our interpretation of the Rosenthal Act.
L118: Further, the conduct that Davidson alleges that the defendants engaged in—i.e., the harassing telephone calls at all hours of the day, and the threats of negative credit reporting and threats to foreclose—is precisely the type of conduct that the Legislature wanted to protect consumers against when it enacted the Rosenthal Act. (See, e.g., Sen. Com. on Judiciary, Rep. on Sen. Bill No. 237 (1977–1978 Reg.
L119: Sess.), as amended May 11, 1977 [&quot;This bill would prohibit any person from engaging in certain debt collection practices&quot; including a &quot;list of prohibited threatening, abusive and deceptive practices&quot;]; Assem. Com. on Judiciary, Bill Digest for Sen. Bill No. 237 (1977–1978 Reg. Sess.), as amended June 24, 1977, hearing date Aug.
L120: 4, 1977 [&quot;This measure governs all debt collection practices arising from the extension of credit if the credit was obtained primarily for personal, family, or household We reject the defendants&#039; attempt to distinguish the debt at issue in Alborzian, which was no longer secured by the property because of the foreclosure process, from Davidson&#039;s debt or a debt secured by a first mortgage.
L121: We see nothing in the Rosenthal Act that would allow debts that were acquired for personal, family, or household purposes to be treated differently under the Rosenthal Act on the ground that one debt remains secured by real property while the other does not.
L122: 15 purposes&quot; and prohibits, among other things, the &quot;caus[ing] a telephone to ring repeatedly&quot;].)
